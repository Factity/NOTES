- The law sought to use US influence as the world’s major nuclear plant and enriched fuel supplier to limit and eventually end civilian plutonium separation and HEU production.
- The policy was inherently discriminatory since nuclear weapon States still needed and separated plutonium for warheads and HEU for submarines.
- Nearly all US nuclear cooperation agreements then required recipients to obtain Washington’s prior consent before reprocessing US-origin spent fuel, enriching uranium above a certain level, or re-exporting US-supplied nuclear items.
- The 1978 Act added new export conditions: generally no US nuclear exports to a non-nuclear-weapon State unless it accepted IAEA safeguards on all its nuclear material.
- For new US supplies, IAEA safeguards must be permanent.
- No nuclear material, equipment, or sensitive technology could be exported to any non-nuclear-weapon State that had terminated IAEA safeguards.
- Prior US consent was also required before reprocessing, enriching, or re-exporting any nuclear material produced using US equipment.
- In effect, the Act called for renegotiating almost all existing US nuclear cooperation agreements.
- It meant prior US consent to reprocessing would be examined stringently case-by-case, with no more general or automatic authorization.
- On 19 October 1977, Carter convened an international conference in Washington to launch INFCE.
- Heads of national nuclear energy agencies and senior officials from some forty nations took part.
- Carter hoped INFCE would temper opposition to the new US policy by showing the “once-through” fuel cycle was less proliferation-prone.
- The US case was weakened by revelations that a Pakistani scientist at a Dutch subcontractor for the Almelo gas centrifuge plant had returned home with plant plans and supplier lists.
- This implied, later confirmed in Iraq and South Africa, that enrichment rather than reprocessing might be the preferred path to nuclear weapons.
- INFCE took place in Vienna from November 1978 to a final plenary on 25–27 February 1980.
- It was a massive operation: 66 countries took part in at least some of its 133 meetings, with the IAEA providing much administrative and technical support.
- But INFCE was a US, not an IAEA, operation, chaired by Professor Abe Chayes of Harvard.
- Chayes, though supportive of Carter’s policy, was impeccably impartial and a very able chairman.
- INFCE produced vast documentation, but many conclusions were soon overtaken by events.
- Its expectations of rapid nuclear power expansion, uranium shortages, price rises, and early breeder reactor use all proved false.
- Despite Carter’s expectations, and despite reprocessing’s direct link to weapon-usable material while the once-through cycle is less proliferation-prone, the debate continued.





Here are a few more bullet points summarizing this section:

- INFCE refused to identify any fuel cycle as more “proliferation-prone” than another.
- Its fundamental conclusion was that a national decision to acquire nuclear weapons is essentially political, not dependent on the fuel cycle chosen.
- But the US Congress, through the 1978 Non-Proliferation Act, had largely pre-empted INFCE’s conclusions.
- INFCE recommended international cooperation on plutonium storage, long-term spent fuel storage, and long-term nuclear supply assurances linked to effective safeguards.
- This led to an IAEA study on international plutonium storage, another on long-term spent fuel storage, and the Board’s Committee on Assurances of Supply.
- The net results were modest: no international plutonium storage system was set up, no nation offered spent fuel storage for others, and the Committee on Assurances of Supply achieved little.
- By 1995, some problems had changed or disappeared: nuclear energy became a buyers’ market, with supplies available provided non-nuclear-weapon importers accepted comprehensive IAEA safeguards.
- All but three importers (India, Israel, Pakistan) and all but one major exporter (China) had accepted that condition.
- The March 1987 UN Conference for the Promotion of International Co-operation in the Peaceful Uses of Nuclear Energy also produced meagre results.
- On 28 March 1979, the Three Mile Island accident in Pennsylvania made headlines worldwide.
- In 1980, the second NPT Review Conference met in Geneva, dominated by nuclear supplies and a comprehensive test ban treaty.
- The NSG Guidelines and the 1978 US Non-Proliferation Act drew sharp criticism from developing countries and Switzerland.
- A consensus report was cobbled together on civilian nuclear use, but no consensus was reached on arms control and disarmament.
- Non-nuclear-weapon States pressed for a comprehensive test ban; the USA and UK resisted, effectively vetoing it.
- The conference ended without a final declaration and amid forebodings about the NPT’s future.
- In June 1980, the IAEA’s Committee on Assurances of Supply began discussing predictable long-term nuclear supplies and the IAEA’s role.
- Main suppliers sought strict export controls; the USA, Canada, Australia, and Scandinavia required comprehensive safeguards as a supply condition.
- Importing non-NPT states sought minimal restrictions, some wanting no IAEA safeguards at all.
- Even among NPT parties, resentment lingered against the 1978 US Act, seen as arbitrary and a threat to supply agreements.
- In September 1979, concern arose over an apparent nuclear explosion signal high over the South Atlantic near South Africa.
- A Carter panel concluded it was probably not a nuclear explosion, but some US and British writers still disagree.
- By the end of 1981, many IAEA programmes were clearly defined and would not change radically for 15 years.
- Funds for nuclear assistance nearly quadrupled from $16,475,000 to $60,300,000, though the technical range and countries remained much the same.
- Severe shocks were in store for safeguards and nuclear safety programmes.
- The NPT transformed the IAEA’s international role, but safeguards still focused almost exclusively on accounting for declared nuclear material.
- This led to intense inspection of countries with most nuclear material—West Germany, other EU states, Japan, and Canada.
- The early 1990s disclosure that Iraq had long operated undeclared nuclear plants showed serious defects in the system.
- Industrialized countries with large nuclear programmes still saw IAEA nuclear safety work as mainly benefiting developing countries.
- It took the worst accident in nuclear history to change their minds and accept nuclear safety as a vital international responsibility.
- The 1981–1997 period saw the end of nuclear power euphoria, the first IAEA safeguards violations, and a sea change in the international political environment.
- The NPT and comprehensive IAEA safeguards were confirmed as permanent features underpinning international security.
- On 26 February 1981, Egypt ratified the NPT, good news for the Treaty’s supporters in a tense region.
- On 7 June 1981, Israeli aircraft destroyed Tamuz 1, the 40 MW(th) materials testing reactor France had built for Iraq at Tuwaitha.
- Israel suspected Iraq planned to use the reactor for nuclear weapons and had tried to dissuade France from supplying it.
- Israeli agents were widely believed responsible for blowing up the reactor core in Toulon and possibly for an engineer’s death in Paris.
- International reaction was harshly critical; it was the first armed attack on a civilian nuclear plant under IAEA safeguards.
- The Director General and Board interpreted the attack as an assault on IAEA safeguards.
- On 8 June 1981, the UN Security Council strongly condemned the attack and called on Israel to pay compensation and place all its nuclear activities under IAEA safeguards.
- The Board strongly condemned Israel and asked the General Conference to consider suspending Israel’s membership rights and privileges.
- In September 1981, the General Conference voted to suspend all technical assistance to Israel and warned of possible suspension of membership rights in 1982.
- Much of the Board’s time in 1981 was spent choosing a successor to Sigvard Eklund, who was ending his fifth term.
- Half a dozen candidates emerged, but the choice became a duel between Hans Haunschild of West Germany and Domingo Siazon of the Philippines.
- The USSR opposed both; votes prevented either from getting the required two-thirds majority.
- Eventually, the USA sounded Sweden, which put forward Hans Blix, an eminent international lawyer and skilled diplomat.
- Blix had been Swedish Foreign Minister and a defender of nuclear power in the 1980 Swedish referendum.
- Haunschild withdrew, but Siazon stayed in for more rounds; finally, on 26 September 1981, Blix obtained the two-thirds majority.
- The Board appointed Blix by acclamation on Siazon’s proposal, and the General Conference approved by acclamation early Sunday morning.
- The appointment of citizens of industrialized countries as the first three Directors General drew sharp criticism from developing countries.
- Undertakings were given to increase their citizens in senior Secretariat ranks and consider their candidates when Blix completed his first term.
- On India’s proposal, the General Conference conferred on Eklund the title of Director General Emeritus by acclamation.
- Within a year, the Israeli attack’s tension tested Blix’s diplomatic skill severely.
- By September 1982, Israel had no intention of complying with the Security Council resolution; its occupation of southern Lebanon sharpened anti-Israeli sentiment.
- Both Houses of the US Congress called for boycotting any UN body that suspended Israel or rejected its credentials.
- Arab States canvassed a draft resolution to suspend Israel’s membership rights, but it lacked the required two-thirds majority.
- Arab delegations then pressed to reject Israel’s credentials, requiring only a simple majority, via an Iraqi amendment.
- In a roll call vote, the votes were evenly divided; the President announced the amendment had not been carried.
- Madagascar then declared it had been present and wished its vote in favour of the Iraqi amendment recorded.
- The IAEA’s chief legal officer said Madagascar had the right to have its position taken into account, and the President ruled its vote valid.
- The US delegate appealed the ruling; the appeal was rejected by three votes, and the Iraqi proposal was adopted by one vote.
- The amended resolution rejecting Israeli credentials was adopted by two votes.
- The UK and US delegations walked out, followed by most Western delegations.
- The US delegate announced Washington would “reassess its policy regarding United States support for and participation in the IAEA and its activities.”
- The USA was by far the main supporter of most IAEA programmes and the largest contributor to its regular budget and technical assistance.
- Its withdrawal would have been the most severe blow any Member State could inflict on the organization.
- The legal advice to the President was questionable, and the substance and timing of the decision could hardly have been worse from Washington’s view.
- Rejecting Israel’s credentials could have set a precedent for similar action at the upcoming UN General Assembly.
- In 1981, Iraq was an NPT party in good standing, the reactor was under IAEA safeguards, and the Israeli attack was the first military strike ever against a nuclear plant.
- The attack was launched when Israel and Iraq were, in practice if not in law, at peace, and was condemned by the USA and almost all UN members.
- The USA had done more than any other nation to create and sustain the IAEA and attached great importance to its safeguards.
- Its willingness to withdraw measured Israel’s influence on US foreign policy.
- The US withdrawal may have been a warning to Arab States not to reject Israeli credentials at the UN and elsewhere.
- Paradoxically, the rejected Israeli credentials were only for the 1982 General Conference; once it ended, Israel could operate as usual.
- The USA, by contrast, withdrew from all participation, though the immediate practical effect was less than if it had happened early in the year.
- It was nevertheless urgent to get the USA back into the Agency.
- With help from the Board Chairman (Ambassador Emil Keblúšek of Czechoslovakia) and US, Soviet, and many other delegations, efforts began to bring the USA back.





1. The US Government suspended participation in IAEA activities over the Israel dispute.
2. Director General Blix made strenuous efforts to persuade the US to reverse its decision.
3. On 14 October 1982, Blix wrote to all Governors affirming Israel remained a fully participating IAEA member.
4. In February 1983, the USA decided to resume participation in the Agency's activities.

5. On 26 April 1986, Chernobyl Reactor Unit 4 exploded, releasing radioactive material across Europe and the entire northern hemisphere.
6. The IAEA and the Soviet Union held an international post-accident review meeting in August 1986.
7. INSAG analyzed the results in September 1986 and issued an updated review in 1992.
8. Chernobyl discredited the Soviet system, devastated the local environment, and harmed public health and mental health in the region.
9. It was the worst blow ever to nuclear power, ending nuclear programmes in several countries.
10. Chernobyl triggered immediate support for expanded IAEA nuclear safety work, including new conventions on early notification and emergency assistance.

11. The Convention on Nuclear Safety was initiated in 1992 by German Environment Minister Klaus Töpfer and Blix, following Chernobyl-era proposals dating to the late 1960s.
12. The convention opened for signature in 1995.

13. From December 1982, the UN General Assembly annually demanded South Africa halt nuclear weapons development and accept IAEA safeguards.
14. From 1987 to 1990, the Board and General Conference debated suspending South Africa's IAEA membership rights.
15. In June 1987, the Board voted 22–12 (1 abstention) to recommend suspension, but the General Conference deferred the decision in 1987, 1989, and 1990.
16. South Africa acceded to the NPT on 10 July 1991 and concluded its safeguards agreement on 16 September 1991.
17. In 1992, the IAEA verified South Africa's Initial Report and found no evidence it was incomplete, with inspectors given "any place, any time" access.
18. In 1993, President de Klerk revealed South Africa had built six nuclear warheads and dismantled all of them in 1989 — making it the only nuclear weapon State to voluntarily scrap its arsenal.
19. The IAEA verified the dismantlement, finding substantial evidence of destruction of weapon components and no missing fissile material.

20. China applied for IAEA membership on 5 September 1983, was approved unanimously on 11 October 1983, and became a member on 1 January 1984; a Statute amendment created a tenth Board seat for it, and in 1989 China agreed to IAEA safeguards on a list of civilian plants — meaning all five nuclear weapon States had by then offered some form of safeguards on their civilian facilities.


21. China’s 1989 IAEA agreement preamble stated its intention to require safeguards on all nuclear exports, but not to make supply conditional on comprehensive safeguards in non-nuclear-weapon states; China joined the NPT in 1992.
22. Germany reunified on 3 October 1990; Bonn informed the IAEA that FRG rights and obligations relevant to the IAEA extended to the former German Democratic Republic.
23. Germany’s EURATOM-related safeguards agreement supplanted the former GDR’s IAEA safeguards agreement.
24. In 1990, Germany announced it would require comprehensive safeguards for new nuclear supplies to any non-nuclear-weapon state; EU members and Switzerland followed, the NSG adopted it, and the 1995 NPT Review and Extension Conference endorsed it.
25. In the early 1990s, Germany tightened national nuclear export controls, partly after disclosures about German involvement in Iraq’s clandestine nuclear programme, and proposed IAEA safeguards strengthening such as universal reporting.
26. In the 1980s, Argentina, Brazil, and Chile radically changed their non-proliferation and IAEA safeguards policies.
27. In November 1990, Argentina and Brazil signed the Declaration on Common Nuclear Policy at Foz do Iguaçu, agreeing to bilateral comprehensive safeguards subject to international verification.
28. In July 1991, Argentina and Brazil established ABACC, a joint agency for accounting for and controlling nuclear materials.
29. Argentina, Brazil, and ABACC negotiated IAEA safeguards covering all nuclear material and activities and relevant nuclear exports; the agreement was signed in December 1991 and entered into force in March 1994.
30. Chile became party to the Tlatelolco Treaty in January 1994; its IAEA safeguards entered into force in April 1995, and Chile acceded to the NPT in May 1995.
31. Cuba signed the Tlatelolco Treaty in 1995; once it ratifies and concludes an IAEA safeguards agreement, the treaty would cover the entire Latin America and Caribbean region.
32. South Africa joined the NPT and dismantled its nuclear arsenal, opening the way for an African nuclear-weapon-free zone; the Pelindaba Treaty text was agreed in 1995 and opened for signature in Cairo in April 1996.
33. France completed nuclear tests in 1995, announced no further tests and dismantled its Mururoa facilities; France, the UK, and the USA signed the Rarotonga Protocol respecting the South Pacific nuclear-weapon-free zone.
34. In December 1995, ten Southeast Asian nations opened the Bangkok Treaty for signature, establishing a Southeast Asian nuclear-weapon-free zone.
35. By the end of 1996, five regional nuclear-weapon-free treaties were in force or under ratification—Antarctic, Tlatelolco, Rarotonga, Pelindaba, and Bangkok—though little progress was made in the Middle East and South Asia.
36. After the 1991 Gulf War, the Security Council asked the IAEA to verify the elimination of Iraq’s ability to acquire nuclear weapons.
37. IAEA inspectors revealed Iraq’s large clandestine nuclear-weapon programme and repeated NPT safeguards violations; the failure to detect it for years sharply criticized the IAEA and threatened safeguards credibility.
38. The IAEA reformed safeguards to better detect clandestine activities and, since 1994, kept inspectors continuously in Iraq to monitor compliance with Security Council resolutions.
39. The DPRK brought its NPT safeguards agreement into force in 1992, but the IAEA could not verify the completeness of its initial report; after the DPRK rejected a special inspection in 1993, the Board found a safeguards violation and reported it to the Security Council.
40. The 1994 DPRK crisis was defused after Jimmy Carter’s intervention and, after Kim Il Sung’s death, the USA and DPRK agreed to freeze and eventually dismantle the DPRK nuclear programme in return chiefly for two US-design power reactors.



41. 1980s concern over sustainable development without environmental damage or resource depletion led to the 1992 Rio UN Conference on Environment and Development.
42. In 1988, the IAEA General Conference asked the Director General to report on the Agency’s contribution to “environmentally sound and sustainable development.”
43. Rio opened the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change and adopted Agenda 21; in 1993 the UN created a Commission on Sustainable Development, and the IAEA became task manager on radioactive wastes.
44. In September 1995, the IAEA Secretariat surveyed its sustainable-development work, highlighting nuclear waste management, nuclear safety, radiation protection, FAO/IAEA biodiversity and water work, health, pollution monitoring, and the Monaco Marine Environment Laboratory.
45. Fossil fuel burning was seen as a major environmental threat; industrialized countries made little progress cutting greenhouse gases, especially CO2, while developing-country emissions rose.
46. An October 1995 Vienna conference, “Electricity, Health and the Environment,” noted nuclear power already reduced CO2 and pollutants, and that hydro and nuclear were the only large-scale low-external-cost options if greenhouse effects were counted.
47. In 1984, major contributors imposed zero real growth on the IAEA regular budget; non-technical-cooperation programmes stayed flat except post-Chernobyl safety funds and tied grants, especially for safeguards.
48. Zero growth caused financial strain as safeguards expanded to cover more material, plants, spent fuel, and the nuclear programmes of South Africa, Argentina, Brazil, Ukraine, and CIS/Baltic states.
49. Some pressure was offset by the IAEA–EURATOM partnership approach and cancelled nuclear projects, such as Germany’s Wackersdorf reprocessing plant and Siemens’ Hanau MOX plant.
50. In 1991, Russia’s non-payment cut regular budget programmes by about 4%; in 1992, all IAEA departments were cut by 13%; Russia resumed payment in 1993, but zero growth continued.
51. Extrabudgetary contributions helped expand core programmes but shifted direction toward donor-country priorities and away from the IAEA’s Governing Bodies and Secretariat.
52. A 1993 US General Accounting Office report said safeguards inspections, equipment purchases, and other activities were deferred or cancelled, and nuclear safety relied on uncertain cost-free experts.
53. After the USSR dissolved, the IAEA from 1992 helped successor states prevent nuclear theft and smuggling and encouraged adherence to the 1987 Physical Protection Convention and IAEA guidelines.
54. Fears that Russian nuclear scientists would sell fissile material or services were largely unfounded, but smuggling incidents rose sharply from 1991 to 1994.
55. In 1994, the first detected attempts to smuggle weapon-usable material occurred: three in Germany and one in the Czech Republic; the largest seizure was 2.73 kg of 87.7% HEU in Prague in December 1994.
56. By the end of 1996, no organized gang or plausible buyers had been found, but public alarm grew; in September 1994 the General Conference urged states to prevent trafficking, with IAEA help on prevention, response, training, and information exchange.
57. The IAEA began collecting media reports on trafficking in 1992, expanded its database in 1995, sought direct information from states, and by late 1995 had 25 nations willing to share information.
58. Confirmed or intercepted smuggling attempts fell from 43 in 1993 and 44 in 1994 to 27 in 1995 and 17 in 1996, though the cause of the decline was unclear.
59. From 1993–1996, 132 incidents were confirmed: 54 in Germany, 22 in the Baltic States, and 10 in Poland; most involved natural, depleted, or low-enriched uranium, and six involved plutonium.
60. No confirmed case involved material significant for diversion or explosive use, and mainly traffickers were put at risk, but controls and monitoring still could not be relaxed.
61. IAEA membership rose from 54 in 1957 to 124 by mid-1997; the 1996 regular budget was $249 million, down from $251 million in 1995, with no real increase.
62. The 1981–1997 period saw the end of the Cold War, nuclear disarmament, the USSR and Warsaw Pact’s dissolution, democracy in Latin America, the end of apartheid, Asian economic growth, and EU progress, though South Asia and the Middle East remained tense.
63. The period also brought Chernobyl, a major setback for nuclear power, credibility challenges from Iraq and the DPRK, IAEA responses, and a financial crisis eased by special contributions.
64. In April 1996, the IAEA, European Commission, and WHO held a major Chernobyl conference ten years after the accident, with over 800 experts from about 70 countries.
65. The Convention on Nuclear Safety entered into force on 24 October 1996; by 30 June 1997, 37 countries were parties, including most nuclear power operators but not the USA.
66. In May 1995, the NPT Review and Extension Conference indefinitely extended the NPT and its safeguards agreements, strengthened review, recognized the IAEA as the competent safeguards authority, and urged detection of undeclared nuclear activities.
67. In 1995 the Board authorized parts of Programme 93+2, and in May 1997 approved a protocol giving the IAEA more information, more intensive inspections, broader access, and greater cost efficiency.
68. The April 1996 Pelindaba Treaty created an African nuclear-weapon-free zone, and the December 1995 Bangkok Treaty created one in Southeast Asia, joining Antarctica, Latin America/Caribbean, and the South Pacific.
69. By end-1996 the IAEA was verifying US surplus HEU and plutonium removed from military use; in September 1996 Russia, the USA, and Director General Blix agreed to explore verifying material withdrawn from military use, and the UN General Assembly opened the CTBT.
70. Since 1990 the NPT regime consolidated and expanded: 45 states acceded in 1990–1996, including China, France, Argentina, and South Africa; five nuclear-weapon-free zones advanced; safeguards were extended to former weapon material; and Hans Blix announced his 16-year term as Director General would end in December 1997.

71. 1944: Fermi and colleagues began work on a small test facility to validate breeding; 20 Dec 1951 EBR-1 in Idaho, 1.1 MW(th), first converted nuclear heat to electricity.
72. 1954 Obninsk, USSR, first commercial nuclear power plant, 5 MW(e), light-water-cooled graphite-moderated; May 1956 Calder Hall, UK, 50 MW(e) gas-graphite, critical; Dec 1957 Shippingport, USA, 90 MW(e) PWR, critical.
73. IAEA Prepcom 1956–57 was sober on nuclear prospects; recommended information exchange, advice, training, evaluation/feasibility studies, and a possible limited reactor programme, but no financing; nothing came of it.
74. From 1958 IAEA promoted smaller reactors for developing countries; “small” then meant up to 50 MW(e), but the definition kept growing.
75. Sept 1958 IAEA General Conference asked for studies of developing-country power needs, small reactor technology/costs, and training; by end-1959, 20 Member States sought IAEA nuclear power advice.
76. 1958 Japan requested 3 tons natural uranium for JRR-3; Canada donated it; Board approved first IAEA supply/project agreements and first IAEA safeguards application; Finland sought TRIGA Mark II and enriched fuel; Austria sought ASTRA fuel.
77. 1961–63 IAEA made first national nuclear power studies for Philippines, Yugoslavia, Pakistan, South Korea, Thailand; Korea, Pakistan, Yugoslavia later built power reactors.
78. 1955 First Geneva Conference heard Obninsk operating experience and prototype reactor reports.
79. Sept 1958 Second Geneva Conference, Eklund as Secretary General, had about 5,000 delegates and 2,150 papers; optimism about cheap nuclear power was flagging; IAEA lacked a commanding UN role.
80. Aug–Sept 1964 Third Geneva Conference, convened by U Thant, focused on nuclear power, began a new nuclear race, and marked UN acceptance of IAEA’s primary civilian nuclear role.
81. 6–16 Sept 1971 Fourth and last Geneva Conference focused on commercialization, integration into economies, and environmental impact; UN and IAEA jointly sponsored it.
82. Early 1960s demonstration reactors operated in leading industrial countries, but nuclear competitiveness remained uncertain; Dec 1963 GE’s Oyster Creek bid was competitive with coal/oil and launched optimism.
83. 1964 Geneva foresaw more than half of some industrial countries’ electricity from nuclear by 2000 and 167,000 MW(e) by 1980, soon raised to 200,000; by 1967 US utilities had ordered over 50 power reactors.
84. 1964 was high tide of optimism for nuclear electricity, desalination, and merchant ships; less than 5% of nuclear capacity was expected outside main industrial countries by 1980.
85. 1973 Arab–Israeli war and oil boycott caused a fourfold oil price rise and record nuclear orders; by 1975 orders fell from 75,000 MW(e) to 28,000 MW(e), which IAEA called temporary.
86. 1975 IAEA forecast 1.0–1.3 million MW(e) by 1990 and 3.6–5.3 million MW(e) by 2000; actual 1995 capacity was 344,422 MW(e), less than one-tenth of the low 2000 estimate; IIASA and OECD/NEA also overestimated.
87. 1965 IAEA joined ENEA in uranium reserve/consumption surveys, excluding Eastern Europe, USSR, China; first joint report Dec 1967; thorium interest in the 1960s, especially India/Brazil, failed because uranium stayed abundant and cheap.
88. By 1981 uranium was in glut; price fell from about $40/lb U3O8 in early 1980 to $23.5–25.0/lb; many workings cut back or stopped.
89. 1970s LWRs became dominant: low-enriched uranium, ordinary water; US-design LWRs in West, Soviet WWERs in East; Germany, Spain, Sweden, Japan preferred LWRs early.
90. France and UK first chose gas-graphite reactors using natural uranium, graphite moderator, CO2 coolant, because LWR economics were uncertain, they lacked enrichment plants, and gas-graphite produced weapons plutonium.
91. France abandoned gas-graphite for LWRs in the late 1960s under Westinghouse licence; UK ordered its first LWR in the late 1980s after building advanced gas-cooled reactors.
92. USSR built WWER and RBMK reactors; exported WWERs only to Warsaw Pact allies, Finland, Cuba; Cuba’s construction was suspended.
93. Canada marketed CANDU heavy-water natural-uranium reactors, built about 20, and sold them to India, Pakistan, Argentina, Romania, China, South Korea; NRX reactors went to India and Taiwan; Indian 1974 explosion used NRX-derived plutonium.
94. Reactor choice had IAEA safety/safeguards implications: on-load refuelled reactors need more inspection; natural-uranium graphite/heavy-water reactors produced most plutonium for test explosions and warheads; DPRK chose gas-graphite, and the 1994 Agreed Framework ended it and substituted two LWRs.
95. 1960s–early 1970s USA and USSR promoted peaceful nuclear explosions (PNEs); the issue shaped NPT and Tlatelolco; India and South Africa used PNE justifications for nuclear explosive technology.
96. NPT Article V gave only five nuclear-weapon States PNE rights; IAEA became the agreed international body for PNE benefits and observation, with guidelines approved in late 1970.
97. IAEA PNE work began 1968; 1972 UNGA asked IAEA to set up a PNE service; 1975 Eklund created a one-official PNE unit and Board advisory group; no formal PNE request ever arose, and it was disbanded.
98. Only USA and USSR, and conceivably India, carried out PNEs; USA abandoned its programme in the late 1970s and USSR carried out its last PNE in the late 1980s.
99. May 1977 IAEA 20th-anniversary Salzburg conference had over 2,000 participants and consensus that fast breeders and reprocessing would eventually be needed.
100. At Salzburg, Joseph Nye announced Carter policy: abandon fast breeder, stop reprocessing/plutonium separation, favour once-through fuel cycle; this foreshadowed Clinch River and Barnwell cancellations and caused prolonged disagreement with Western Europe and Japan.
101. From late 1960s IAEA sought 100–500 MW(e) plants for developing countries; 1968–69 survey badly overestimated 1970–85 developing nuclear growth; 1972 WASP survey and Sept 1973 results suggested about 100 plants of 600 MW(e)+ in 14 countries.
102. A 1974 IAEA Bulletin article claimed oil at $6–7/barrel would make 100 MW(e) nuclear competitive and over 205 orders possible in 44 developing countries, including Uganda and Liberia.
103. Manufacturers preferred larger plants; most developing buyers had grids for 500–1000 MW(e); India was the exception, building 220 MW(e) heavy-water reactors.
104. 1984–87 surveys found many smaller designs below 600 MW(e) on offer, but buyers hesitated over economics; only China’s 300 MW(e) PWR and Pakistan’s Chashma unit materialized; interest shifted to desalination and district heating, with China planning 200 MW(th) district heating and Russian projects stopped by public resistance.
105. Nuclear desalting drew strong 1960s–early 1970s interest: US “Water for Peace,” US–USSR agreement, MEND idea, USA/Mexico plant plan, and Soviet BN-350 at Shevchenko/Aktau producing electricity and desalted water.
106. Oct 1965 IAEA desalting symposium heard nuclear desalting cost about six US cents/m3 versus one to two cents for agriculture; large projects were shelved except the Soviet/Kazak plant; interest revived in late 1980s–1990s for potable water, and a 1995 North African study found it feasible and cost-competitive.
107. Three Mile Island in March 1979 helped dry up US orders; 14 reactor orders were cancelled in 1979 and more later.
108. Until Chernobyl in 1986 Western politicians still affirmed nuclear confidence, including the 1981 G-7 Ottawa summit; construction starts fell from 18 in 1985 to 4 in 1986 and zero in 1995.
109. 1980s cancellations/stops included Germany’s Wackersdorf reprocessing plant in 1989 and a large Soviet Siberian reprocessing plant; fast breeder projects declined.
110. East Asia contrasted: Japan grew from 33 plants/23,665 MW(e) in 1985 to 51/39,893 MW(e) in 1995; South Korea from 3/2,720 MW(e) to 11/9,120 MW(e), with more under construction.
111. China brought its first three nuclear plants into commercial operation in 1994; Indonesia planned up to eight; Thailand and Viet Nam showed interest; IAEA involvement in Japan was minimal except OSART 1988/1995 and safeguards, but it ran many training/safety/siting activities in China, Indonesia, Korea, Thailand.
112. Fusion research was declassified in 1958; IAEA began Nuclear Fusion journal in 1960, monthly from 1978, held first plasma/fusion conference Salzburg 1961 and biennial conferences thereafter; 16th was Montreal Oct 1996, next Yokohama 1998.
113. Late 1960s tokamak interest grew; IAEA created International Fusion Research Council in 1970 and launched INTOR workshops in 1978; conceptual design developed in 1981 and updated in 1983/1985.
114. Nov 1985 Reagan–Gorbachev summit encouraged expanded fusion co-operation; April 1988 EU, Japan, USSR, USA launched ITER under IAEA auspices; conceptual design completed Dec 1990; engineering design began July 1992, expected six years.
115. Fusion offers abundant deuterium/lithium fuel, no greenhouse gases, no criticality accidents, but has radioactive tritium/activated materials; commercial use likely around mid-21st century; no formal ITER construction commitment and funding doubts remained.
116. IAEA published Directory of Nuclear Reactors 1959–1976 and annual reactor operating reports from 1971; in 1980 launched PRIS, the authoritative world databank on civilian power reactors.
117. Since 1981 PRIS supported Nuclear Power Reactors in the World; PRIS went online in 1989 and Internet in 1995; by 1997, 76 users in 33 Member States; MicroPRIS from 1991 used by over 200 organizations in over 50 states and 9 international organizations.
118. Since 1992 IAEA created country profiles on economic, energy, electricity, industrial and organizational nuclear power characteristics; 30 of 32 states with nuclear plants contributed.
119. Since early 1970s IAEA developed computer tools for energy/electricity planning: WASP, MAED, ENPEP, VALORAGUA, BIDEVAL, and DECADES.
120. DECADES, with eight other international organizations, assessed technical, economic, environmental and health trade-offs across full energy chains; its reference database covered about 300 typical facilities and country database about 25 countries and 2,500 facilities.
121. IAEA used standing international working groups of about 25 experts meeting every 1–2 years; four dealt with general issues: life management, instrumentation/control, training/qualification, and water reactor fuel performance/technology.
122. Four IWGs dealt with reactor types: light water, gas cooled, heavy water, and liquid metal cooled/fast breeder; advanced designs emphasized longer grace periods and passive safety.

- Future nuclear power and IAEA programmes depend heavily on future electricity demand, especially in Asia, where demand growth and nuclear prospects are strongest.

1. Nuclear safety deals with nuclear fuel cycle risks; radiation safety with ionizing radiation uses; waste management with radioactive waste and disposal; “nuclear safety” is used as an umbrella term.
2. IAEA’s statutory work covers research, information exchange, standards/regulations/guides, helping Member States strengthen infrastructure, and promoting binding international conventions.
3. The IAEA Statute gave it the right to require health and safety measures in safeguarded projects and to have inspectors determine compliance.
4. The Prepcom foresaw safety inspectors; the first health and safety measures, approved 31 March 1960, allowed up to two safety inspections a year of assisted operations.
5. In practice, IAEA safety requirements applied only to Agency projects and technical cooperation projects, not normally to safeguards.
6. Board decisions in 1959–60 separated safeguards inspection from safety measures; no safety inspectors were formally appointed, and early 1960s inspections were ad hoc.
7. In February 1976, the Board replaced routine compliance inspections with advisory safety missions by state agreement; the IAEA effectively waived its statutory routine verification right.
8. In 1958, the IAEA began collecting Member State nuclear safety and waste practices and regulations and began a manual of safe practices for isotope users.
9. Early 1960s safety work focused on international recommendations, guides, and standards, mainly at Headquarters, to help countries lacking national rules.
10. By end-1961, the IAEA had issued eight sets of recommendations, including research reactor operation, radioisotopes, sea disposal, and transport.
11. Board-approved transport regulations came in September 1960, were published in 1961, and were recommended to Member States and international bodies.
12. In June 1962, the Board approved Basic Safety Standards for Radiation Protection based on ICRP recommendations; they were revised in 1967, the early 1980s, and later.
13. IAEA direct reactor safety began with analysis of the fatal Vinča reactor accident in Yugoslavia in October 1958 and a safety analysis of Japan’s JRR-3.
14. The Vinča analysis led to an IAEA publication on all unclassified reactor accidents.
15. In 1960, the IAEA arranged an international safety evaluation of Switzerland’s DIORIT research reactor, the first of many such evaluations.
16. On 10 March 1961, the IAEA agreed with Monaco and the Oceanographic Institute to study radiation effects in the sea; this later became ILMR and then IAEA-MEL.
17. In June 1963, the Board approved the first international nuclear accident assistance agreement, between the IAEA, Denmark, Finland, Norway, and Sweden; it was signed in October 1963.
18. Early IAEA safety staff promoted nuclear power’s safety and environmental advantages; there were no serious radiation casualties at civilian power plants until Chernobyl.
19. Late 1960s public concern and the 1972 Stockholm Conference began affecting IAEA work; the 1970–71 Annual Report first mentioned public debate on nuclear energy and environment.
20. An August 1970 IAEA/USAEC New York symposium concluded nuclear power stations polluted far less than other thermal power and released trivial radioactive doses to the public.
21. Environmental debate often ignored naval reactors; Copenhagen rejected nuclear power and complained about Sweden’s Barsebäck but not nuclear submarines passing nearby.
22. The Soviet Union dumped high-level naval reactor waste off Novaya Zemlya while opposing Western low-level sea dumping.
23. Public attitudes toward nuclear power changed before Three Mile Island and Chernobyl, especially in the USA and Western Europe, affecting IAEA work.
24. After Stockholm 1972, the UN created UNEP in Nairobi in 1973; the IAEA obtained special funding in 1973 to expand safety work and sought UNEP help.
25. In 1974, the IAEA launched the Nuclear Safety Standards (NUSS) programme: codes and safety guides for safe design, siting, and operation of current power reactors.
26. Some Western European experts initially resisted NUSS, fearing disguised US standards; the IAEA planned eventually to extend NUSS to fast breeders and the fuel cycle.
27. By Three Mile Island, the IAEA had published five NUSS Codes and ten Safety Guides, with 39 more prepared or in preparation.
28. NUSS began during a nuclear boom with 25–35 orders a year; the IAEA chose international technical principles and guides rather than a standing team for each project.
29. The IAEA agreed that five NUSS Codes and 47 Safety Guides should be prepared between 1975 and 1980.
30. In 1974, the Board decided NUSS documents would be recommendations, not legally binding; later debates rejected mandatory status, and a 1987 questionnaire showed 47 states generally conformed.
31. In 1992, nuclear safety convention drafting avoided referencing NUSS codes to prevent “petrification” of standards likely to change.
32. On 28 March 1979, Three Mile Island’s reactor core partially melted; there was no significant radiation release beyond containment and no injuries, but immense psychological and industry damage.
33. In 1979, worldwide nuclear capacity on order fell about 8,000 MW(e); eight new plants were ordered and 14 previous orders were cancelled.
34. Three Mile Island and Chernobyl occurred in the two nuclear weapon states that had most promoted civilian nuclear energy but had taken early arms-race shortcuts.
35. After Three Mile Island, Eklund convened safety experts who recommended specialized meetings, expanded research and information exchange, and emergency and technical assistance.
36. Experts also urged states to publish safety research quickly, require emergency plans before licensing, negotiate assistance agreements, test plans, and let the IAEA routinely check safety work.
37. Brazil, West Germany, and Sweden proposed reviewing IAEA safety work; West Germany urged comparing safety concepts, and Sweden urged harmonizing national rules and offered to host a meeting.
38. The first post-accident Board welcomed expansion if 1979 was financed by voluntary contributions but was guarded on adding professional posts and regular budget for 1980.
39. France, West Germany, Canada, and the UK stressed that national authorities bear ultimate responsibility for nuclear safety.
40. Three Mile Island showed a less experienced state would need international help; the Secretariat prepared recommendations for prompt notification and mutual assistance.
41. Eklund’s proposal for conventions on notification and assistance was rejected; the Board thought guidelines sufficed, but Chernobyl later showed guidelines did not prevent delay.
42. The Board agreed to share Three Mile Island lessons; governments became more broad-minded, but change was slow until a worse accident.
43. Three Mile Island immediately expanded NUSS; the IAEA accepted Sweden’s offer for an October 1980 Stockholm conference on current nuclear power plant safety issues.
44. The Stockholm conference had about 700 specialists and policy makers; it found no safety factors limiting nuclear use but identified machine–man interaction as weak, urging better training and controls.
45. In November 1979, the Director General reported that all Member States had been informed of IAEA help to incorporate codes and guides; the Secretariat studied emergency needs and compiled an expert roster.
46. Three Mile Island taught that a major accident was real, not remote; Western nuclear establishments took lessons, and the USA set up INPO to improve operating safety.
47. Three Mile Island hastened transfer of nuclear safety responsibility in some countries from national nuclear energy agencies to independent regulatory authorities.
48. In the 18 years after Three Mile Island, OECD and developing countries with nuclear plants had no serious accident, though coolant leaks attracted media attention.
49. The Soviet Union seemed to learn little from Three Mile Island, partly because it was a closed society with a command economy.
50. The IAEA long tried to create a global accident/incident reporting system but met confidentiality and proprietary resistance; OECD/NEA began its IRS in 1978.
51. Three Mile Island gave IRS impetus; a two-year trial began in January 1980, NEA approved it at end-1981, and the IAEA extended it in 1983.
52. The IRS aims to bring safety-significant incidents to operators, regulators, constructors, and designers for analysis and improvement.
53. In 1986, the UK, Canada, and Yugoslavia joined the IRS; today virtually all nuclear power states are in the system.
54. IRS annual reports ranged from 231 in 1985 to 87 in 1984, averaging 150–160; since 1996 NEA and IAEA jointly operate it; by 1997, 31 states selected events, and AIRS held 2,522 reports.
55. In 1982, the IAEA published its first annual nuclear safety review for 1980–81, summarizing Three Mile Island lessons on design, controls, training, and emergency planning.
56. The reviews featured ICRP dose limitation—no increase unless net benefit, ALARA, and absolute limits—incorporated into revised Basic Safety Standards in 1982 for ILO, NEA, and WHO.
57. The 1983–85 reviews covered natural radiation, creation of INSAG, OSARTs, technical safety, and analyses of Sizewell B, Superphénix, Kalkar, a Japanese APWR, and a German HTGCR.
58. The 1986–88 reviews were dominated by Chernobyl; from 1989 the review was incorporated into the IAEA Yearbook.
59. Chernobyl on 26 April 1986 had far-reaching repercussions; analyses involved Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, the IAEA, INSAG in 1986 and 1992, the International Chernobyl Project in 1990–91, and “One Decade After Chernobyl” in 1996.
60. Causes were first blamed on operators, but later analyses blamed RBMK design defects, the Soviet system, lack of safety culture, earlier Leningrad 1975 and Chernobyl 1982 accidents, and poor command responsibility.
61. The Chernobyl test sought to see if a turbogenerator could power the plant during rundown; power was reduced to 700 MW(th), the test began at 01:06 on 25 April, and the explosion occurred at 01:24 on 26 April.
62. Operators withdrew control and safety rods, switched off safety systems, and violated rules; power fell to 30 MW(th), returned to 200 MW(th), then surged to 100 times nominal, causing fuel rupture, steam explosion, and a second explosion.
63. INSAG concluded the accident flowed from deficient safety culture throughout Soviet design, operating, and regulatory organizations.
64. “One Decade After Chernobyl” in Vienna, 8–12 April 1996, reported 116,000 evacuated in 1986, an exclusion zone of 4,300 km², 210,000 more resettled since 1990, and 200,000 liquidators.
65. Health effects included 237 hospitalized, 134 with acute radiation syndrome, 28 deaths in three months, two other Unit 4 deaths, 14 later deaths, about 800 child thyroid cancers, three thyroid deaths, and major mental stress.
66. Cancer estimates for 7.1 million residents were about 6,600 fatal cancers over 85 years versus 870,000 spontaneous; UNSCEAR estimated the highest European regional average committed dose at 1.2 mSv over 70 years to 2056, against natural 2.4 mSv/year.
67. No sustained severe non-human impacts were found; milk and green vegetables were initially contaminated, collective farm food was below limits by 1996, but game, berries, and mushrooms will exceed caesium-137 levels for decades.
68. Design deficiencies contributing to Chernobyl were essentially remedied and repetition seemed impossible; other deficiencies lagged; the sarcophagus stability was doubtful, with worst-case site personnel exposure but no expected widespread effects beyond 30 km.
69. Politically, Chernobyl weakened the Soviet system, encouraged glasnost, and strained the economy; some countries stopped nuclear expansion, but Russia and Ukraine continued at reduced pace, Armenia reopened a reactor, Belarus and Kazakstan considered plants, and Czech/Slovak and French programmes were largely unaffected, with France exporting electricity.
70. The IAEA responded promptly: Blix visited Moscow in May 1986 and was the first non-Soviet to inspect from the air; the Board approved expert meetings, two conventions, a special General Conference, and a safety programme review; the Early Notification Convention entered into force on 27 October 1986 and the Assistance Convention on 26 February 1987; Brazil made the first assistance request in 1987; later work covered intervention levels, Codex Alimentarius, FAO/IAEA farm protection, RBMK safety programmes, the International Chernobyl Project, INSAG, OSARTs, and post-Chernobyl safety services.





71. OSARTs review management, organization, operations, maintenance, technical support, radiation protection, chemistry, and emergency planning, using international standards, INSAG safety-culture reports, and team experience.
72. An OSART typically has 5–6 nuclear safety experts from various countries plus 2–3 IAEA staff, spends about three weeks at a plant, and sends findings and recommendations to the government.
73. First OSART went to the Republic of Korea in 1983; 1984 missions went to Yugoslavia and the Philippines; 1985 missions went to Brazil, France, Pakistan, and the Philippines again.
74. Chernobyl sharply increased OSART requests, increasingly from industrialized countries; missions grew from 6 in 1986 to 11 in 1989, then 5–7 a year in 1990–95; by end-1995, 79 OSARTs and 31 follow-ups had reviewed 69 plants in 28 countries.
75. ASSETs screen and analyse safety-significant events from operation, surveillance, and testing; the first went out in 1986; by mid-1995 there had been 61 ASSETs for 19 states and 66 training ASSETs for 28 states.
76. ESRS provide advice on engineering safety of operating or planned reactors, including site choice, external hazards, accident management, and ageing; most assessments covered existing plants, especially WWERs.
77. ESRS found Soviet-designed reactors lacked structural earthquake resistance; about two-thirds of 99 ESRS to 24 countries from February 1989 to mid-1995 assessed seismic hazards in the former USSR and Eastern Europe.
78. Bulgaria, Slovakia, Hungary, and Armenia strengthened plants after ESRS; Pakistan arranged seismic reviews of Chasnupp and Kanupp.
79. IPERS-PSA began in 1988 to arrange independent international reviews of probabilistic safety assessments; by mid-1995 there had been 35 reviews, focused on WWERs but also the Netherlands, Korea, Sweden, Switzerland, and China.
80. INSARRs assess research reactor safety; they began in 1972, often required by project or supply agreements, and examine safety reports, operation, maintenance, training, radiation protection, and startup/shutdown.
81. INSARRs first visited industrialized countries Finland and Norway in 1987; by mid-1995 there had been 123 assessments in 37 Member States, free for developing countries; weak points included outdated safety documents, poor quality assurance, and incomplete procedures.
82. In 1994 the IAEA began drawing up safe-practice recommendations, an inventory of large gamma irradiators, safety checklists, a worldwide survey, and an international reporting system on accidents and unusual events.
83. ASCOTs help states assess and improve nuclear safety culture; by early 1995 there were 24 services, mostly seminars, plus reviews in the UK, Netherlands, and South Africa; deficiencies included weak policy, poor training, and superficial event explanations.
84. IRRTs review national nuclear safety regulations and enforcement; the first IRRT visited Brazil in 1988.
85. WANO was formed in 1988–89 by nuclear power plant managers to improve operational safety through links and information exchange; it chose London for headquarters and held its first meeting in Moscow in May 1989.
86. Chernobyl raised doubts about RBMK and older Soviet reactors, especially WWER-440/230; on 21 September 1990 the General Conference approved a comprehensive nuclear safety resolution covering NUSS, OSART/ASSET, INES, Chernobyl assessment, and WWER-440/230 safety.
87. In 1990 IAEA missions investigated WWER-440 problems at Greifswald, Bohunice, and Kozloduy; after reunification Germany decided to dismantle five Greifswald WWER-440/230 plants.
88. In 1992 the IAEA extended safety work to RBMKs and in 1993 to WWER-440/213 and WWER-1000 plants; by 1993 it covered all Russian and Eastern European nuclear power plants and coordinated with G-24 aid.
89. After Chernobyl, INSAG issued INSAG-1 in 1986 and the revised INSAG-7 in 1992; INSAG-3 on Basic Safety Principles for Nuclear Power Plants appeared in 1988 and helped start the Convention on Nuclear Safety.
90. NUSS was completed in 1986 with 5 Codes and 55 Safety Guides covering governmental organization, siting, design, operation, and quality assurance; attention then shifted to helping states implement them.
91. Ageing became a major issue: by the late 1990s over 200 power plants would have operated 20+ years; in 1995 over 40% of research reactors were over 30 years old; since 1972 to end-1995 there were 123 research-reactor safety missions in 37 countries.
92. Basic safety standards were first issued in 1962, revised in 1967 and 1981–82; after ICRP 1990 recommendations, a joint secretariat began revision in 1991, and the Board approved revised standards in 1994, including lower occupational dose limits.
93. The standards assume a linear dose–effect relationship at low doses with no threshold, based largely on Hiroshima/Nagasaki high-dose data; new research and epidemiology prompted debate, a Seville conference in November 1997, and US–Russian Mayak studies.
94. INES classifies nuclear events from Level 1 to Level 7; Levels 1–3 are incidents and 4–7 accidents; Chernobyl was Level 7; it began a trial in 1990, had 25 states using it by end-1990, and 59 Member States by mid-1997.
95. Technical cooperation in nuclear safety grew from about 8% of the programme after Three Mile Island to over a quarter by the 1990s; by 1995 there were over 150 projects, and 1980–95 over 5,000 people were trained in nuclear safety.
96. Eastern European dependence on nuclear electricity was high: Lithuania 83.44%, Slovakia 44.53%, Bulgaria 42.24%, Hungary 42.30%, Slovenia 37.87%, Ukraine 37.8%, and the Czech Republic 20.1%.
97. IAEA gave technical assistance to Armenia, Bulgaria, Czech Republic, Lithuania, Russia, Slovenia, Slovakia, and Ukraine to upgrade WWER safety; after Russia stopped requiring spent-fuel return, waste management became pressing.
98. With EU support, the IAEA helped Croatia, Hungary, Romania, Slovakia, and Ukraine prepare nuclear safety and waste legislation and establish regulatory bodies; in 1994 it prepared a basic programme for Belarus, Estonia, Kazakstan, Latvia, Lithuania, Moldova, and Uzbekistan.
99. IAEA–EU missions helped Romania before its first reactor startup; Model Projects helped Slovakia establish a regulatory body and Ukraine apply international standards; IAEA also helped Bulgaria’s Kozloduy earthquake resistance, Hungary’s Paks training, and Ukraine reduce Chernobyl-related food radioisotopes.
100. A novel 1994 Hungarian project built a mock WWER-440/213 reactor from unused parts of abandoned or cancelled Soviet-design plants to train Paks staff and personnel from seven other countries.
101. NUSAC was established by G-24 donor and recipient countries in 1992 to coordinate nuclear safety assistance; the IAEA acted as technical adviser.
102. In 1994 the IAEA completed three interregional projects: strengthening radiation safety and acceptance of basic safety standards; improving nuclear waste management procedures; and advising on emergencies and reducing radiation exposures.
103. A first 11-year project assessed radiation safety in 64 developing countries using RAPATs; WAMAP over eight years advised 42 countries on waste from power/research reactors, uranium mining and milling, and radioisotopes; a third project helped with nuclear emergencies and medical radiation protection.
104. In 1989 the Safety Series was restructured into Safety Fundamentals, Safety Standards, Safety Guides, and Safety Practices; later it became the Safety Standards Series and Safety Reports Series.
105. Three Safety Fundamentals appeared in 1993–96: The Safety of Nuclear Installations, The Principles of Radioactive Waste Management, and Radiation Protection and the Safety of Radiation Sources.
106. New advisory bodies were created: ACSS as the top standards advisory body; NUSSAC for nuclear safety; RASSAC for radiation safety; WASSAC for waste safety; and TRANSSAC for transport safety.
107. The Convention on Nuclear Safety was proposed after Three Mile Island and Chernobyl; in September 1991 Germany’s Klaus Töpfer proposed it, Blix supported it, drafting began in 1992, it was approved in June 1994, opened for signature in September 1994, and entered into force on 24 October 1996.
108. The Convention on Nuclear Safety is the first legally binding instrument on land-based civilian power reactors; responsibility rests with the state, which must have a legislative framework, independent regulator, licensing, inspection, enforcement, and safe siting, design, construction, and operation.
109. Parties must review safety after entry into force, upgrade urgently or shut down if needed, hold review meetings at least every three years, submit reports, and use the IAEA as secretariat; by 18 April 1997, 37 states had ratified.
110. Historically, the Statute treated safeguards and safety similarly, and the Prepcom suggested associating safeguards and health/safety inspections; in 1961 the Board separated them, and in 1976 it replaced health and safety inspections with voluntary advisory missions.
111. After Three Mile Island and especially Chernobyl, the IAEA became proactive in nuclear safety, binding conventions, legal frameworks, safety services, and assessing design shortcomings.
112. Radioactive waste comes from the nuclear fuel cycle, medicine, industry, research, weapons production and testing, and accidents; public concern focuses on waste entering the food chain or contaminating people.
113. A 1982 IAEA collection of authoritative 1975–81 reports concluded that safe radioactive waste disposal means were available and tested, and that public fears were politically and psychologically inflated.
114. Early waste practice included Western European low-level dumping in the Atlantic, secret weapons waste handling, French and UK gas-graphite spent-fuel reprocessing, and French La Hague reprocessing of LWR fuel from 1976.
115. Under Carter Administration pressure, the US industry abandoned LWR spent-fuel reprocessing and stored fuel at reactors or away-from-reactor sites; the NEA organized ocean dumping until it was tacitly abandoned in 1982.
116. IAEA waste work covered marine radioactivity research, information exchange, and help to countries, especially developing ones; setting uniform standards was harder than reactor safety standards because geology, climate, population, and infrastructure differ.
117. Major waste meetings included Monaco 1959 on radioactive waste disposal, Vienna 1962 on high-level waste treatment and storage, and three 1975 environmental symposia on radioactive/non-radioactive releases, aquatic releases, and plutonium/transuranics.
118. The 1983 Seattle conference was the first IAEA meeting to cover the full range of waste issues—technological, environmental, regulatory, institutional, legal, economic, social, and policy—with over 500 participants.
119. Under the 1972 London Convention, the IAEA defined high-level wastes not to be dumped at sea; definitions were revised in 1978 and 1986; a moratorium began in 1983, dumping was formally prohibited in 1993, and the ban entered into force in 1994.
120. WAMAP began in 1987; INWAC was set up in 1989; RADWASS covered planning, disposal, near-surface and geological disposal, mining/milling waste, and decommissioning; in 1990 the Board approved RADWASS standards and the first international consensus on underground high-level disposal.
121. In September 1990 the General Conference adopted the Code of Practice on the International Transboundary Movement of Radioactive Waste and asked the Director General to monitor it.
122. In 1994 the IAEA reviewed waste documents and prepared a Safety Fundamentals on radioactive waste disposal principles and a Safety Standard on national nuclear waste programmes, both published in 1995; it also issued Minimum Acceptable Waste Infrastructure criteria.
123. WATRP began in 1989 for peer review of national waste projects; earlier reviews were in Sweden 1978–87 and the UK 1988; later missions went to Korea 1991, Finland 1992, Czech Republic 1993, Slovakia 1993, Norway 1994, and France’s Centre de l’Aube in 1996.
124. Other field activities included cleaning radioactively contaminated sites, safe reactor decommissioning, and centralized storage for radium sources; radium in cancer therapy was largely replaced by caesium-137.
125. In 1994 the General Conference asked for a convention on safe radioactive waste management; experts completed the draft in April 1997, and the Director General recommended a diplomatic conference on 1 September 1997 and signature at the 41st General Conference.
126. Waste technical cooperation included over 20 training courses in 1991–95 and regional courses in Africa, Latin America, South East Asia, and Europe; projects also addressed WWER-440 waste minimization and decommissioning.
127. Regional or multinational disposal sites would reduce locations of high-level waste and spent fuel, but few countries accept foreign waste; the USSR required return of spent fuel from supplied reactors to prevent plutonium extraction, and the USA had a similar policy for HEU-origin fuel.
128. A late-1970s plan to dispose of Austrian Zwentendorf waste in Egypt collapsed after Egyptian media outcry; this shattered the idea that poorer countries would readily accept waste for payment; in 1994 the IAEA studied a regional arrangement for African spent radium sources.
129. The worst waste legacy came from the US and Soviet weapons programmes: Soviet waste was discharged into rivers, oceans, and ground; naval reactors were dumped in the Kara Sea and Western Pacific; US cleanup was estimated at $189–265 billion over 70 years or more.

57. The maritime conventions applied the same central principle: the operator bears sole responsibility.
58. This principle was intended to protect victims and clarify liability.
59. It also encouraged carriers and operators to participate in nuclear transport.
60. Overall, the passage shows a long struggle to reduce duplication, harmonize interpretation, and build a more unified international nuclear liability system.





**IAEA Safeguards Challenges & Reforms — 70 Key Points**

**Special Inspections**
1. Under standard safeguards agreements, the IAEA was obligated to ensure safeguards applied on all nuclear material within a State's territory, jurisdiction, or control.
2. The agreements placed no limits on IAEA access during "special inspections" — inspectors could go to any place in the State.
3. This reflected Article XII.A.6 of the Statute, giving inspectors "access at all times to all places and data."
4. Before a special inspection, the IAEA needed the State's agreement; if refused, the Board could order admission forthwith.
5. If the State still refused, the Board would likely report to the Security Council, General Assembly, and all Member States that it could not verify non-diversion.
6. Until the Gulf War, the IAEA had never conducted a special inspection at an "additional" location.
7. Such inspections could detect undeclared material — or refusal would ipso facto incriminate the State, as happened with the DPRK.
8. Even wide inspection powers were useless without knowing where to look — a UN agency cannot operate like a secret service.
9. Improving unclassified data flow and IAEA analysis would help, but access to "national technical means" (satellites) was also needed.
10. Until 1991, governments feared sharing intelligence data would reveal sources or satellite capabilities; the USA accepted this risk for Iraq and the DPRK.
11. The Statute's clauses providing the IAEA direct access to the Security Council were activated for the first time by the Gulf War, in both directions.

**Iraq: How Close to the Bomb?**
12. Rolf Ekeus estimated in 1991 that Iraq could have had enough fissile material for a device in just 12–18 months.
13. Estimates were uncertain — full capacity of the electromagnetic isotope separation plants, feedstock, and electricity supply were unproven.
14. Other open questions: whether Iraq could have mastered explosive manufacture, detonation at designed yield, and delivery means.
15. In April 1992, nuclear weapon designers from the USA, Russia, UK, and France met at IAEA Headquarters to review inspectors' collected documents.
16. They concluded Iraq was at least three years — possibly more — from a first crude nuclear weapon due to significant bottlenecks.
17. The first breach of an IAEA safeguards agreement came via unsuspected clandestine plants, not diversion of declared material.
18. The IAEA was seen by many as failing its first diversion detection test.
19. Without the Gulf War, the programme might have been discovered only when Iraq openly demonstrated a bomb.
20. This judgement was unduly harsh — the Director General, staff, Action Team, and Board acted swiftly and decisively — but a fundamental safeguards review was essential.
21. The review was promptly undertaken and first applied in the DPRK, eventually producing "Programme 93 + 2" (1993–1997).

**Lessons from Iraq**
22. A "significant quantity" is 25 kg of uranium (>90% U-235) or 8 kg of plutonium-239.
23. Iraq held over 25 kg of HEU, but distributed between two reactors, so neither held a significant quantity.
24. Inspection frequency was geared to the material in each plant, not the total in the country or centre.
25. Iraq had only two inspections per year, though the two Tuwaitha reactors' fuel could have been amalgamated for a weapon — as its crash programme (revealed in 1995) planned.
26. The IAEA increased inspection frequency in the few other centres with similar material distribution.
27. If total material at a research centre equals a significant quantity, that should determine minimum inspection frequency — meaning at least four annual inspections for pre-war Iraq.
28. More frequent inspections might have detected the crash programme attempt within three months instead of six.
29. Another option: reduce the "significant quantity" values, which derive from a mid-1960s UN study.
30. Lower thresholds could have raised inspection frequency from twice a year to monthly, but with global budgetary consequences and effects on the timeliness goal.
31. The Iraq problems were not unique — the Chemical Weapons Convention, Biological Weapons Convention, and CTBT could face similar challenges.
32. A determined, authoritarian, well-funded State with a skilled nuclear establishment could defy the NPT and evade detection for years.
33. During the Iran–Iraq war, Western tilt towards Iraq plus Soviet support may have aided the evasion.
34. Even with the physical programme eliminated, Iraqi scientists retained invaluable knowledge of fissile material production and warhead construction.
35. No non-proliferation regime will ever be completely foolproof — this argues for strengthening IAEA safeguards, not removing them.

**1992 IAEA–EURATOM Partnership**
36. The 1973 IAEA–EURATOM agreement produced friction, duplication, and 1977 "joint teams" that failed to eliminate waste.
37. The 1973 agreement required the IAEA to inspect EURATOM non-nuclear-weapon States less frequently than similar non-EURATOM States — in practice the opposite often occurred.
38. Blix was told inspection intensity at one EU fuel fabrication plant was at least three times that at a similar Japanese plant.
39. Partly this stemmed from "theological" attachment to doctrines banning delegation of responsibility.
40. In 1992, the Director General and the EU Commissioner signed a "new partnership approach."
41. The new approach replaced "principle of observation" and "joint teams" with the rule "one job, one person" — one inspector per agency, each drawing independent conclusions.
42. The agencies also agreed to cooperate in research and training, share laboratories, and use techniques not requiring inspector presence.
43. The IAEA expected to cut routine inspection in EU non-nuclear-weapon States by one half to two thirds.
44. Person-days in these States fell from about 3000 (1991) to 1200 (1995), though about 700 of the reduction was due to plant closures.
45. Germany's 1989 abandonment of the Wackersdorf reprocessing plant and mid-1990s abandonment of Hanau MOX plans also reduced inspection forecasts.

**DPRK Violation of Safeguards**
46. The DPRK acceded to the NPT on 12 December 1985 — reportedly a Soviet condition for power reactor supply that never materialized.
47. Article III.4 requires a comprehensive safeguards agreement within 18 months of accession; the DPRK took no action and set political conditions.
48. On 10 April 1992 — nearly five years overdue — the DPRK finally brought its safeguards agreement into force.
49. The Initial Report (4 May 1992) listed surprises: a 5 MW(e) graphite-moderated reactor, a fuel fabrication plant, a reprocessing plant, and two larger reactors (50 and 200 MW(e)) under construction.
50. The indigenous Magnox reactors resembled those the UK used for its first plutonium warheads; the 50 MW(e) unit (due 1995) could produce 40–50 kg of plutonium yearly — enough for five to ten warheads.
51. The DPRK showed under 100 g of plutonium, claiming a single 1990 reprocessing campaign.
52. IAEA analysis showed several campaigns had occurred — meaning more separated plutonium than declared.
53. US satellite images revealed two undeclared waste-storage structures, camouflaged with trees.
54. The DPRK refused IAEA visits, claiming the sites were military; Blix formally demanded a special inspection, which was promptly rejected.
55. On 25 February 1993, the Board endorsed Blix's request with a three-month deadline; on 12 March the DPRK announced NPT withdrawal.
56. On 1 April 1993 the Board found the DPRK in breach and reported to the Security Council, which on 11 May voted 13–0 (2 abstentions: China, Pakistan) to "invite" compliance.
57. On 11 June 1993, a day before withdrawal took effect, the USA persuaded the DPRK to suspend it and accept normal inspections of the seven declared sites.
58. Through 1993 and into 1994 the DPRK continued to frustrate and harass inspections.
59. In 1994 the IAEA proposed a structured fuel discharge to verify reactor core history; in May the DPRK rejected this and discharged fuel in a way that made analysis virtually impossible.
60. On 10 June 1994, the Board suspended all technical assistance to the DPRK, which on 13 June gave notice of withdrawing from the Agency.
61. On 16 June 1994, the USA proposed escalating sanctions; the DPRK warned sanctions meant war; tension mounted sharply.
62. On 17 June 1994, former President Jimmy Carter went to Pyongyang and met Kim Il Sung, returning with conciliatory messages.
63. Just as the USA responded, Kim Il Sung died.
64. On 18 October 1994, the two sides announced the "Agreed Framework," signed 21 October; the Security Council asked the IAEA to carry it out on 4 November, and the Board authorized the Director General on 11 November.

**Agreed Framework Key Terms**
65. The DPRK would freeze its nuclear programme, accept international verification, and eventually dismantle all frozen plants; the IAEA would verify the freeze.
66. Fuel from the 5 MW(e) reactor would be stored and disposed of without reprocessing in the DPRK.
67. The USA would organize an international consortium ($4 billion) to supply two 1000 MW(e) light water reactors; dismantling would finish when the LWR project was completed (target 2003).
68. The USA would supply heavy oil to offset energy lost to the freeze; both nations would ease trade restrictions and move toward diplomatic relations; the USA gave formal assurances against nuclear threat.
69. The DPRK would stay in the NPT and come into full safeguards compliance — including verifying its Initial Report — before delivery of key nuclear components.
70. Notably, the Framework did not mention the DPRK rejoining the IAEA; the Republic of Korea would bear over $2 billion of the cost and supply the plants, with Japan and the USA covering most of the rest.

**Criticism and Outcome of the Agreed Framework**
71. Most of the world greeted the Framework with relief — the danger of a second Korean war was averted.
72. Supporters argued it was based on verification, not trust — any deviation would halt all commitments, and LWRs would make the DPRK dependent on foreign fuel.
73. US critics alleged the DPRK got the better deal — 2000 MW(e) of reactors, fuel oil, and progress toward recognition in exchange for stopping what it shouldn't have done — and warned it would encourage imitation.
74. No credible alternative emerged, and most critics reluctantly accepted it.
75. The IAEA was unhappy about a five-to-seven-year delay before inspecting the suspect waste sites and fully verifying the Initial Report.
76. The Framework did not strengthen the IAEA's inspection rights; the confrontation over the first-ever special inspection demand remained unresolved.
77. By end-1995 the IAEA still could not verify the completeness of the DPRK's Initial Report, and the DPRK remained in formal breach, as the General Conference noted in September 1995.
78. The dispute showed the Security Council reluctant to enforce its own 31 January 1992 declaration that proliferation of WMD is a threat to peace warranting "appropriate measures."
79. The DPRK's relative impunity risked undermining IAEA deterrence, the Council's enforcement authority, and compliance with multilateral arms control treaties generally.
80. The USA in effect paid the price for halting plutonium separation, choosing to live with uncertainty over past amounts rather than accept continued separation — but the intact reprocessing plant gave the DPRK residual leverage.

**IAEA Successes Tested in the DPRK**
81. Sophisticated analysis detected a mismatch between presented plutonium and waste, exposing the understatement.
82. The Board reaffirmed the right to conduct special inspections at undeclared locations.
83. High-quality satellite imagery convinced the Board of probable undeclared waste stores — a precedent for IAEA access to national intelligence.
84. The Board acted promptly and decisively, confirming the special inspection demand within four days and thrice finding breach and reporting to the Security Council.
85. For the first time outside Iraq, the Board used the IAEA's direct line to the Security Council over a deliberate safeguards violation.
86. However, the special inspection procedure proved highly confrontational, and the DPRK successfully resisted any such inspection.

**Programme 93 + 2**
87. In 1991–1992, Blix secured three essentials: Board reaffirmation of special inspection rights; better information flows including national intelligence; and Security Council backing (the 31 January 1992 presidential declaration).
88. The Council never converted the declaration into a formal, binding commitment.
89. Environmental sampling — tested in Iraq — proved highly sensitive for detecting undeclared material and reprocessing; the DPRK case confirmed its value.
90. In February 1993, on an EU proposal, the IAEA established voluntary expanded reporting of exports/imports of nuclear material and exports of specified equipment and non-nuclear material.
91. The 1971 system audited declared material accounts well but could not verify the completeness of State declarations — the core lesson of Iraq and the DPRK.
92. South Africa provided the IAEA's first comprehensive completeness assessment experience after its 1991 NPT accession.
93. In December 1993 the Board endorsed launching "Programme 93 + 2"; in March 1995 it endorsed the direction, requiring assurances of both correctness and completeness.
94. May 1995: the NPT Review Conference gave the programme its general blessing.
95. In June 1995 the Board approved measures not requiring new legal authority; the second part requiring legal amplification went to the Board in December 1995.
96. A special open-ended committee drafted the protocol over 55 meetings, completing work 4 April 1997; the Board approved it on 15 May 1997.
97. Some EU countries objected that the proposals were too intrusive, risked proprietary information, created legal/constitutional problems, and were discriminatory against non-nuclear-weapon States.
98. The IAEA must hold the same inspection authority in all States with comprehensive agreements; what seems intrusive in one case is essential in another.
99. A fissile material cut-off treaty plus Programme 93 + 2 acceptance by the nuclear weapon States for their volunteered plants would reduce NPT discrimination.

**Programme 93 + 2 Main Proposals**
100. Additional access in declared plants beyond pre-defined "strategic points" during routine inspections — avoiding the confrontation of special inspections.
101. Information on and access to all buildings within a nuclear site perimeter (as at Tuwaitha, where 80+ structures included clandestine weapon activities).
102. Information and limited access to plants without nuclear material but involved in fuel-cycle-related manufacturing (e.g., enrichment component plants).
103. Cost efficiency: sharper, focused safeguards to permit reduced inspection at routine plants like LWRs, given zero-growth budget constraints.

**Growing Demands on Safeguards**
104. The Soviet collapse extended IAEA safeguards for the first time to Ukraine, Belarus, Kazakhstan, Armenia, other CIS Republics, and the Baltic States.
105. Additional safeguards were applied in Argentina, Brazil, and South Africa.
106. Warhead dismantlement released large plutonium and HEU stocks; the USA began placing some under IAEA safeguards.
107. On 11 May 1995 the NPT was extended indefinitely, automatically making all related comprehensive safeguards agreements permanent.

**Material Under Safeguards (End of 1995)**
108. 45.1 tonnes of separated plutonium, of which 11.0 tonnes (~1300 significant quantities, roughly 1300 warheads) in non-nuclear-weapon States under comprehensive agreements.
109. 4408.5 tonnes of plutonium in irradiated fuel; 4 tonnes of recycled plutonium in fuel elements.
110. 20.4 tonnes of HEU (608 SQs), of which 10.0 tonnes (~300 SQs) in non-nuclear-weapon States.
111. 47,260 tonnes of low enriched uranium; 104,395 tonnes of source material.
112. Only separated plutonium and HEU are directly weapons-usable, making the ~11 tonnes of separated plutonium and ~10 tonnes of HEU in non-nuclear-weapon States the most significant figures.

**Laboratory Support**
113. From the early 1960s, the IAEA laboratory analyzed uranium and plutonium samples from safeguarded plants; NPT entry into force caused rapid growth in samples.
114. The Safeguards Analytical Laboratory (SAL) at Seibersdorf was completed in 1975 as part of a planned Network of Analytical Laboratories (NWAL).
115. Successful network tests ran in 1972–1973 (12 labs analyzing German plant samples) and 1974–1975 (EUROCHEMIC input solutions from Mol, Belgium).
116. NWAL began operating in 1975 with ~480 samples; by the mid-1990s SAL alone analyzed about 1500 samples yearly, much of it for Iraq operations and Programme 93 + 2.
117. A "clean room" for environmental monitoring was completed at SAL in 1995.

**Delays in Concluding Safeguards Agreements**
118. By end-1996, 63 non-nuclear-weapon NPT parties had not concluded safeguards agreements; over 50 were more than 18 months overdue, some by 20–25 years.
119. The situation was less serious than statistics suggest: most were small African or Central American developing countries with no significant nuclear activity.
120. Only five overdue States had safeguardable material or plants; three were covered by other agreements (Tlatelolco or special agreements — Argentina, Colombia, Ukraine).
121. Algeria was covered by INFCIRC/66/Rev.2 safeguards; Georgia was negotiating.
122. Still, a third of NPT parties being formally non-compliant gave pretexts for delay — as the DPRK had done for nearly five years.
123. A possible fix: States without safeguardable plants/material could formally notify the IAEA on accession and conclude an agreement only if they later acquire such items.

**Financing of Safeguards**
124. Originally safeguards were funded from the IAEA's regular budget, all members paying on the standard scale.
125. In 1971, anticipating post-NPT cost rises resisted by developing countries, the Board and General Conference approved special financing arrangements.
126. "Shielded" countries (per capita income below one third of the average of the ten largest contributors) paid substantially reduced shares from 1972.
127. In 1976, shielded contributions were frozen to placate the G-77.
128. In 1978 the USSR briefly fell into the shielded category for one year.
129. In 1980 the ten largest contributors became ineligible for shielding, and the threshold shifted to one third of the 15 highest per capita income countries.
130. In 1989 a new system unfroze shielded contributions for price increases, extended in 1992 for 1993–1995 budgets.
131. In 1995 the Board adopted a complex revision under which poorer members would eventually pay half their standard-scale share toward safeguards, to be reviewed by 2000.

**Global Plutonium Stocks**
132. Post-INFCE attempts in the late 1970s–early 1980s to create an international plutonium storage scheme under Article XII.A.5 failed; by the early 1990s political will was still lacking.
133. The five nuclear weapon States plus Germany, Japan, Belgium, and Switzerland began informal transparency meetings with the IAEA on plutonium.
134. Surplus weapons HEU can be blended down into LEU reactor fuel, rendering it non-explosive; separated plutonium retains explosive potency.
135. Two main disposal options for surplus plutonium: MOX fuel burn in power reactors, or vitrification with fission products and deep underground disposal — both hard and costly to reverse.
136. From 1993 the IAEA built a database on civilian separated plutonium and confidence-building measures on its handling, storage, and disposal.
137. Short reports on plutonium production, use, and stocks appeared in the IAEA Yearbooks for 1995 and 1996.
138. Supply/demand mismatch was projected to last until about 2001, with civilian stocks peaking near 220 tonnes around that year if MOX use grew.
139. Uncertainties included MOX demand growth and the fate of over 100 tonnes of plutonium potentially recovered from dismantled warheads.

**Safeguards: The Situation Today**
140. The Cold War's end transformed the safeguards environment — expanding operations to former Soviet republics among other changes.
141. Without the Cold War's end, Security Council agreement on Iraq, the DPRK, and the January 1992 declaration would likely have been impossible.
142. South Africa's denuclearization — driven by the changed security environment — removed the main obstacle to an African nuclear-weapon-free zone and encouraged similar zones elsewhere.
143. US–Russian disarmament opened the way to the NPT's indefinite extension and permanent safeguards agreements.
144. The IAEA and its safeguards were major beneficiaries of the Cold War's end, having served as a bridge between superpowers since the early 1960s and pioneered institutionalized on-site inspections that helped end the Cold War itself.






**IAEA Technical Co-operation Programme — 100 Key Points**

**Overview & Chief Beneficiaries**
1. For most developing Member States, nuclear electricity generation, heating, or desalination remained a distant prospect.
2. Their main benefit from nuclear energy was small-scale applications in agriculture, health, industry, environment, hydrology, and research, plus research reactors for education and radioisotope production.
3. The scientific infrastructure needed for these applications is far less demanding than for a nuclear power programme.
4. Many smaller-scale uses were still beyond the least technically advanced countries.
5. At end-1996, the IAEA had 20 Member States in the UNDP "least developed countries" (LDC) category.
6. Most country programmes and research support flowed to developing nations already technically advanced.
7. In 1976, only 16 of 98 aid recipients since 1958 had received over $1 million each — all relatively populous, nuclear-advanced developing countries.
8. Thirty-six developing countries had received less than $250,000 in total assistance.
9. From 1986–1996, 20 developing countries each received over $6 million in technical co-operation, while 31 received under $1 million.
10. The top 20 recipients (1986–1995) included 7 operating nuclear power states (Brazil, Bulgaria, China, Hungary, Pakistan, Mexico, Romania), 3 building/planning plants (Cuba, Indonesia, Iran), and 2 that abandoned plans post-Chernobyl (Egypt, Poland).
11. Only two LDCs — Bangladesh and Tanzania — were among the top 20 recipients.
12. Eighteen of the top 20 operated or were constructing research reactors; Cuba and Tanzania were the exceptions.
13. Notable absentees from the top 20: Iraq and Yugoslavia (formerly significant recipients) and Argentina and India (for reasons explained below).
14. The IAEA made special efforts for less advanced States via infrastructure projects, scientist training, and regional programmes benefiting non-participants.
15. Example: isotope hydrology traced Sahel underground water resources even where nations couldn't yet participate scientifically.
16. Example: the sterile insect technique eliminated the New World Screwworm that invaded Libya in 1988, benefiting all neighbours regardless of participation.

**Scientific Support Structure**
17. Since the late 1950s, three technical Divisions in the Department of Research and Isotopes supported isotope/radiation applications in developing countries.
18. These Divisions ran Co-ordinated Research Programmes, promoted information exchange, and provided lab services and training.
19. Since 1989–1990, two Divisions in the Department of Technical Co-operation developed and administered the programme (regional programming; delivery of experts, fellows, equipment, courses).
20. The Seibersdorf laboratories trained numerous developing-country scientists in instrument calibration, dosimetry, and environmental sample analysis.
21. The Marine Environment Laboratory at Monaco (MEL), unique in the UN system, also trained fellows from developing countries.

**Defining "Developing Country"**
22. In September 1984, delegations questioned the Secretariat's meaning of "developing country" regarding staff representation recommendations.
23. The Director General sought G-77 views, summarized to the Board on 11 June 1985.
24. The G-77 referred to lists attached to UN General Assembly resolutions rather than defining the term.
25. The Board found a precise definition an academic, unrewarding exercise; absence of one rarely caused problems.

**Eligibility & the Single UN Aid Question**
26. All Member States are eligible for IAEA assistance unless debarred by the General Conference or Security Council.
27. In practice, OECD and richer nations do not seek Technical Co-operation Fund (TCF) financing.
28. The document asks why the IAEA shouldn't channel all aid through UNDP instead of its own TCF.
29. Under such a system, recipients rather than donors would allocate resources between sectors; the IAEA would act as executing agency.
30. The UN family moved toward more, not less, financial independence from each other.
31. Centralized planning proved ill-suited to 150+ heterogeneous countries at all development levels.
32. In UNDP planning, powerful national constituencies in agriculture, health, and education corner most funds.
33. The IAEA's UNDP-funded programme shrank over two decades from nearly half to a few percent of the total programme.
34. The sharp 1992 drop in resources reflected the devaluation of the rouble, a major donor currency.
35. Politically, richer countries raised TCF targets to make safeguards budget growth more acceptable to the G-77, and shielded poorer States' safeguards contributions.

**IAEA's Distinctive Position**
36. Donor discontent with UN agency duplication and management — US arrears, US withdrawal from UNESCO, withdrawals from UNIDO and threatened UK/Australian/German ones — spared the IAEA radical surgery.
37. The IAEA's sound management reputation, valued safeguards/safety missions, and concrete development benefits protect its programme.

**Evolution of the Programme**
38. Technical assistance emerged as the IAEA's major programme when safeguards, nuclear material supply, and nuclear power all underperformed expectations, retaining its lead until the late 1960s.
39. In 1958, the IAEA began sending multidisciplinary "Preliminary Assistance Missions" (PAMs).
40. PAMs covered medicine, agriculture, hydrology, nuclear power, uranium mining, and research reactors.
41. By end-1959, PAMs had surveyed 40 developing Member States; over 50 countries were visited in total.
42. The Prepcom identified lack of trained personnel as the chief limiting factor, so fellowships and training courses took priority.
43. The 1958 General Conference set a $250,000 voluntary contribution target; 218 fellowships were awarded that first year.
44. By the mid-1990s, about 1,000 fellowships and ~1,800 training course participants were funded annually.
45. The Statute balances "special needs of the underdeveloped areas" against "sovereign equality of all members" — all Member States are eligible for TCF help.
46. This benefited Eastern European nuclear science, as citizens were eligible for coveted fellowships prized by Western hosts cut off from Eastern colleagues for ~20 years.
47. The USA offered 20–30 consultants, full 1958 fellowship costs, a radioisotope lab, two mobile isotope labs, and a small reactor (offer not taken up).
48. The first mobile laboratory arrived October 1958, used in Austria, Greece, Yugoslavia, Germany, then East Asia; the second (December 1959) served Mexico and Argentina.
49. The 1958 programme covered only fellows; 1959 added experts, equipment, visiting professors, and training courses.
50. After debate, the IAEA joined the UN Expanded Programme of Technical Assistance (EPTA) in 1958.
51. In 1963, IAEA-funded fellowships were reserved for students from States receiving EPTA country-programme assistance.
52. In 1960, the Board approved "Guiding Principles and General Operating Rules," later embodied in a Supplementary Agreement each recipient signs.

**Projects, Safeguards & the NPT Controversy**
53. Until 1977, TCF projects were small — at most twelve man-months of expert services plus equipment; multi-year projects were UNDP-funded.
54. In 1978, multi-year projects entered the IAEA's own programme, with closer project monitoring recommended.
55. In September 1977, the Board decided safeguards would normally apply to projects making a "substantial contribution" to a "sensitive technological area" (enrichment, reprocessing, heavy water, plutonium, MOX).
56. This echoed London Club/NSG Guidelines and was sharply criticized by Governors from non-NPT States.
57. Since 1977, the IAEA has given no technical assistance aiding national programmes in sensitive technological areas.
58. In 1979, revised Guiding Principles updated the misuse prohibition from "further any military purpose" (Statute language) to the NPT formula barring manufacture or acquisition of nuclear weapons or explosive devices.
59. The 1979 vote was 22 for, 3 against (Argentina, Brazil, India), 4 abstentions (Guatemala, Republic of Korea, Tanzania, Venezuela).
60. India declared it "no longer interested" in IAEA assistance and has not participated (except some training courses) since 1979.
61. Argentina withdrew from the programme (returning in 1991); Brazil and Pakistan signed the Revised Supplementary Agreement only case-by-case, in 1991 and 1994 respectively.
62. The dispute was principled, not practical — agriculture, medical, or hydrological aid could hardly build weapons, though trained experts or IAEA-found uranium theoretically could contribute.
63. In 1983, the Board's first policy review directed the Secretariat to help developing States set priorities; systematic evaluation of project impact began that year.
64. A 1987 evaluation of 63 projects found effective response and technology transfer, but flagged training gaps and shortages of national counterpart staff.
65. In 1986, a shift from one-year to two-year programming was tested successfully and made permanent in 1991.

**Conceptual Evolution**
66. By end-1996, over 19,000 scientists, engineers, and technicians had been trained under fellowships; 1,558 training courses were held from 1980–1996.
67. By the late 1980s the institution-building phase was complete; the programme shifted to direct, measurable impact on high-priority economic and social needs.
68. Goal: partnership in development — e.g., new crop varieties in farmers' hands rather than new publications from institutes.
69. Concept 1 — "Model Projects" (Board approved first 12 in 1993, 11 more in 1994): must meet a well-assessed national need, deliver significant end-user achievement with end-user involvement from the start, use nuclear tech only with distinct advantage, and be sustainable via strong government commitment, with close IAEA monitoring.
70. Concept 2 — "Country Programme Framework": IAEA and government agree on a few priority areas capable of significant impact, pointing to Model Project opportunities.
71. Concept 3 — thematic/sectoral planning: demonstrate nuclear techniques' efficiency versus non-nuclear alternatives and identify which countries can use them; final inclusion remains the country's choice.

**Primacy of Safety**
72. Radiation protection was the first operational thematic plan, since basic safety standards are a precondition for all ionizing radiation work; waste management and plant safety followed.
73. Chernobyl and the Soviet breakup brought new Member States with urgent nuclear safety needs.
74. Except in Romania, Eastern European and CIS reactors followed Soviet safety standards, inferior or incompatible with international ones — conspicuously lacking containment domes.
75. The IAEA helped upgrade these plants, particularly WWER reactors, to international standards (detailed in Chapter 7).

**Help to Dynamic Nuclear Programmes**
76. The programme helped South Korea in the late 1970s–early 1980s plan its programme and build its safety infrastructure; similarly for Spain earlier.
77. China adopted the entire NUSS (Nuclear Safety Standards) body by translating it into national legislation.
78. An OSART mission judged the Chinese-designed Qinshan reactor — the first built on Chinese soil — fully meeting international standards.
79. The IAEA trained large numbers of Chinese nuclear engineers and safety experts.

**Programme Resources**
80. Four funding sources: the TCF (voluntary contributions, now dominant); extrabudgetary funds (UN-family bodies like UNDP, plus earmarked "footnote a/" research contributions); contributions in kind (experts, fellowships, equipment); and the regular budget (administration, technical officers, IAEA share of research contracts — not, in principle, for aid to individual States).

**Funding History & Shortfalls**
81. The TCF target rose from $125,000 (1958) to $1.5 million (1959), then stalled at $2 million from 1962–1970.
82. A persistent gap emerged between targets, pledges, and payments — e.g., 1959 pledges of $1,183,044 against a $1.5 million target, with only $875,000 paid.
83. Recurrent shortfalls prompted repeated proposals to fund technical assistance through the assessed regular budget.
84. Non-convertible currency contributions created disposal problems for the Secretariat, and experts/scientists were reluctant to accept payment or training in some currencies.
85. In 1961–1962, the General Conference urged each State to contribute at least its regular-budget percentage; many complied, but since 1984 the main problem is the target–pledge gap.
86. Total resources grew eightfold in nominal terms from 1960 to 1980 ($2.5M to $20.9M); the TCF grew from $1M (1960) to ~$13.3M (1980).
87. In 1974, pledges exceeded the target by 2.8% and payments exceeded pledges.
88. In 1978, a $2.1M convertible-currency deficit matched a $2.1M non-convertible surplus; by 1980 the surplus fell to $542,000.
89. "Footnote a/" projects (unfunded sound requests published for donors) grew from ~$400,000 (1976) to $7,079,880 (1996) — letting donors selectively aid NPT parties without IAEA discrimination.
90. Egypt's 1980 proposal (backed by India) to fund assistance via the assessed budget was strongly resisted by the USSR, USA, Western Europe, Australia, and Japan; the working group failed, as did later attempts.
91. Resources peaked at ~$50 million in 1989 (TCF $44.7M); the USSR/Russia's inability to pay caused declines in 1990 and especially 1992.
92. Over 80% of requests were included in the 1993–1994 biennium; 1995 resources rebounded to ~$63.35M (TCF $60.3M).
93. Careful income estimates and monthly monitoring meant the IAEA never cancelled or deferred an approved project for lack of funds.
94. In 1995, the Board encouraged seeking bilateral development agency and international financial body co-funding, especially for Model Projects.

**Distribution & Forms of Assistance**
95. Until the mid-1980s, 15–20% of disbursements went to each of four areas: food and agriculture; medicine; physical and chemical sciences; radiation protection and nuclear safety; industry/earth sciences took over 10%.
96. In 1994, nuclear safety led with 22.9%, followed by food and agriculture (22.4%), physical/chemical sciences (17.3%), human health (13.1%), and industry/earth sciences (12.1%).
97. Nuclear power-related assistance rose in the late 1960s–1970s, dropped after Three Mile Island (1979), briefly recovered in 1984–1985, then declined after Chernobyl (1986).
98. The IAEA has never been directly involved in building any nuclear power plant or major fuel cycle plant, though it was nominal supplier for plants in Mexico and Yugoslavia.
99. The programme now sends ~2,000 experts yearly across ~150 subjects; equipment has held at ~40% of costs since 1971 (vital for poorer States); fellowship placement through government channels can take 18 months.
100. Regional co-operation flourished — the Cairo training centre (inaugurated 1963), the RCA for Asia (1972), ARCAL for Latin America (in force 1982), and AFRA for Africa (in force 1990) — promoting South–South co-operation, resource sharing, and knowledge pooling, with 17 RCA, 19 ARCAL, and 21 AFRA members by end-1996.


101. The ICTP was established as a unique institution; the idea arose in the late 1950s to promote East–West co-operation, required little costly equipment, was proposed at the 1960 General Conference, and was approved by the Board for Trieste in June 1963.

102. The ICTP began work in October 1964 with a four-week plasma physics seminar; it hosted conferences, seminars, workshops, and training courses, and by the 1990s received about 4,000 scientists yearly, with more than 40 Nobel Prize winners taking part.

103. Under Abdus Salam, the ICTP created associate membership and federation schemes; by 1995 there were about 300 Federation Agreements; Salam was Director until 1993, then President, and Miguel Virasoro succeeded him in June 1995.

104. Italy and Trieste gave generous support; UNESCO became joint sponsor in 1970 and contributed equally with the IAEA; other donors included Denmark, Germany, Japan, the Netherlands, Sweden, the USA, the EU, OPEC, and UNDP; UNESCO took over administration at end-1995, but the IAEA remained a partner.

105. The ICTP became a model for similar institutions in industrialized and developing countries; UNESCO coined “Triestino” for institutions modelled on it, and it was praised as a clearing house, a crossroads for physicists, and a way for scientists to penetrate the Iron Curtain.

106. In 1958, the IAEA set up a provisional Headquarters laboratory in the basement of the former Grand Hotel; its small physics and chemistry sections analysed air, milk, and vegetation for fallout, prepared and distributed radioisotope samples for standards, and launched a global tritium measurement programme.

107. In April 1959, the Board approved a permanent laboratory at Seibersdorf near Vienna; it began operation in October 1961 as the first full-fledged truly international laboratory, designed for comparison and coordination and to support IAEA programmes rather than conduct independent nuclear research.

108. Seibersdorf underpinned IAEA work in environment, medicine, agriculture, hydrology, nuclear safety, and safeguards; after the 1964 FAO/IAEA Division, it expanded into agriculture and nutrition, with a plant breeding unit in 1965, entomology unit in 1968, new agriculture wing in 1986, training facility in 1990, and entomology extension in 1997.

109. From 1961 to 1963, Seibersdorf measured radioactivity in food and milk from atmospheric and ground nuclear tests by the USA, USSR, UK, and France; this ended after most atmospheric testing stopped in 1963.

110. Since 1961, the hydrology section supplied tritium water standards to many countries for the IAEA’s worldwide hydrogen and oxygen isotope survey, establishing a crucial basis for IAEA hydrology projects.

111. In the early 1960s, Seibersdorf began analytical quality control after UNSCEAR found alarming discrepancies in national laboratory fallout data; the IAEA arranged intercomparisons and reference materials, mid-1960s results were “impressively bad,” and by 1974 a comprehensive service was offered.

112. The range of reference materials expanded to sea water, sediments, marine biota, and hydrological materials; partners included the French CEA, the EU’s Central Bureau of Nuclear Measurements, and the US New Brunswick Laboratory; by 1995 the catalogue had about 1,600 reference materials, and the services were used after Chernobyl.

113. Seibersdorf held its first international training course in 1963 on determining radionuclide content of food; since then it has run three to four international courses yearly on agriculture, hydrology isotopes, instrumentation maintenance, and radiochemical analysis, plus hands-on training for developing-country scientists.

114. The Monaco Laboratory’s origins lay in the 1958 UN Law of the Sea resolution asking the IAEA to help control radioactive discharges into the sea; a 1958–59 IAEA panel proposed limits, and in November 1959 the IAEA held the first international conference on sea disposal of radioactive wastes in Monaco.

115. In 1961, the IAEA, Monaco, and the Musée Océanographique under Jacques Cousteau began a three-year research programme on radioactivity in the sea; this led to the International Laboratory of Marine Radioactivity, which studied radionuclide distribution, marine organisms, and radiation effects on marine life.

116. The Monaco Laboratory served as an umbrella institute, providing sea water, sediment, and marine life samples with measured isotopes for calibration; in 1975 an intercalibration involved 110 laboratories in 27 IAEA Member States; the political rationale was concern over low-level sea dumping, criticized by Cousteau, while the USSR criticized the West but dumped high-level waste in the Arctic and Far East.

117. In 1974, UNEP joined the IAEA and Monaco as a principal funder; the Laboratory broadened to non-nuclear contamination, became a service laboratory for UNEP and the leading international centre for marine radioactivity, created a Marine Environmental Studies Laboratory in 1986, and was renamed the IAEA Marine Environment Laboratory in 1991.

118. IAEA-MEL’s later work included the 1991 Gulf War oil pollution, Black Sea radioactivity, the International Arctic Seas Assessment Project with five expeditions finding insignificant contamination, Far Eastern seas joint expeditions, Mururoa French test studies finding extremely low radioactivity, ocean global-warming research, the Danube basin study finding it radiologically clean except Chernobyl fallout, northern Adriatic PCB and mercury work, Caspian Sea studies, the GLOMARD database, and new Monaco premises completed in 1994.

119. In the early 1960s, several Board members, including representatives of the USSR, France, and the UK, were sceptical about the IAEA laboratories and the ICTP, arguing they duplicated work that national laboratories could do better and more cheaply.

120. Experience proved this scepticism ill-founded: the laboratories and ICTP earned a fine scientific reputation and provided valuable services, and Russia and France, once particularly dubious about Trieste, Seibersdorf, and Monaco, found them most useful in assessing the environmental impact of their own former or recently discontinued military nuclear operations.



121. Radiotherapy success depends on precise dose delivery; too small a dose is ineffective, too large may cause severe complications or death, so accurate dosimetry is essential.
122. In the 1960s, patients at many clinics worldwide were irradiated without adequate dose control, alarming the IAEA and WHO.
123. The IAEA and WHO agreed to an extensive training programme and to enlarge the pilot dose-verification system to cover all major world regions.
124. Since 1962, the IAEA has helped laboratories, hospitals, and clinics in Member States calibrate radiation dose measurement equipment.
125. In 1963, the IAEA acquired the world’s first portable system for measuring absorbed dose from high-energy X rays, cobalt-60, and betatron electron beams.
126. From 1963 to 1970, that portable system was sent to radiotherapy centres and research laboratories in Belgium, Czechoslovakia, Germany, Hungary, Switzerland, the USSR, and the UK.
127. In 1968, the IAEA began a service for accurate measurement of radiation from cobalt-60 therapy units and medical accelerators used in cancer treatment.
128. In 1966, thermoluminescent dosimeters became available; the IAEA began an international postal distribution service sending about 300 sets per year.
129. In 1968, WHO joined the project; the IAEA dosimetry laboratory prepared and evaluated the dosimeters while WHO distributed them to hospitals and institutes in developing countries.
130. By end-1996, IAEA services had verified 3,000 radiotherapy beams; a similar service measured cobalt-60 radiation used in food irradiation and medical supply sterilization.
131. Following a December 1974 WHO/IAEA meeting in Rio de Janeiro, the agencies decided to help member nations set up a world network of Secondary Standard Dosimetry Laboratories (SSDLs).
132. The SSDL network aimed to ensure patient radiation doses were measured according to international standards; the IAEA distributed ionization chambers and dosimeters for precise therapy beam calibration.
133. A 1976 IAEA/WHO Working Arrangement linked primary standards bodies, such as the International Bureau of Weights and Measures, with SSDLs responsible for calibrating dosimeters in participating countries.
134. Since 1976, the IAEA has served as the central point in the network, helped developing countries build SSDLs, and trained their staff under its technical cooperation programme.
135. In 1979, the IAEA built its own dosimetry laboratory at Seibersdorf, serving as a model medium-sized SSDL for developing countries.
136. By end-1995, 69 laboratories and 6 SSDL national organizations in 57 countries were in the network, plus 16 affiliated members including primary standards laboratories.
137. Nuclear medicine is perhaps the most common radiation application in developing and industrialized countries, using minute radioactive molecules of known biological behaviour as tracers.
138. These radiopharmaceuticals act as guided molecular probes, administered in vivo or added in vitro as in radioimmunoassay, seeking recognition sites in targeted cells.
139. Gamma-emitting probes can be detected externally and measured, giving functional and biochemical data; a gamma camera produces two-dimensional images of radiotracer distribution.
140. Beta-emitting radionuclides at physiological concentrations can destroy targeted tissue, turning the molecular probe into a precise molecular guided missile against cancerous tumours.
141. The IAEA has played a significant role in introducing and promoting nuclear medicine in developing countries; by 1995, over 95% of IAEA developing Member States had nuclear medicine services.
142. A 1995 IAEA survey found 2,172 gamma cameras installed in 78 developing countries.
143. Following the late 1960s development of radioimmunoassay in the USA and UK, the IAEA supported projects in Singapore in 1972 and in Sri Lanka and Kuwait in 1974.
144. In 1986, the IAEA began its first regional project on radioimmunoassay of thyroid-related hormones, involving 123 laboratories in 13 Asia-Pacific countries.
145. From 1986 to 1995, the IAEA supported 51 technical cooperation projects on radioimmunoassay in 34 Member States and 7 regional projects involving over 300 laboratories.
146. As developing-country laboratories became more expert, some primary reagents were produced locally and, after testing, replaced costly imported articles.
147. The IAEA helped introduce external quality assessment schemes in Asia, Latin America, and Africa as a final arbiter of assay reliability.
148. Between 1986 and 1995, 568 scientists in Latin America, 161 in Asia and the Pacific, and 90 in Africa were trained in radioimmunoassay and related topics.
149. Results included reduced radioimmunoassay costs, more assays per medical personnel, improved reliability, and better local or regional reagent availability.
150. Two-thirds of participating laboratories reduced processing costs for a common hormone to less than 30% of imported commercial kit costs; sophisticated reagent producers cut single-test costs by 90% or more, with clinical workloads rising two- to fivefold.
151. By 1995, IAEA assistance had largely ceased, but sustainable screening programmes were established in Thailand, where 80% of one million annual live births are screened for hypothyroidism, with full coverage expected by 2000.
152. In the 1990s, the IAEA extended its programme to therapeutic uses; a Coordinated Research Programme enabled hospitals in developing countries to use strontium-89, phosphorus-32, and samarium-153 for intractable bone pain from cancer metastases.
153. Iodine-131, traditionally used for hyperthyroidism, was also employed to enhance patient response to treatment.
154. Nuclear medicine includes radioactive DNA probes or genetic markers to identify specific DNA bits, amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) from a single cell sample.
155. In the early 1990s, the IAEA promoted PCR for detecting Chagas’ disease in Latin America, malaria in Africa, and other communicable diseases such as meningeal tuberculosis, lepra, schistosomiasis, and leishmaniasis.
156. In the 1990s, the IAEA introduced the technique to detect genetic or hereditary diseases such as cystic fibrosis, thalassemia, and haemophilia.
157. The IAEA’s human health programmes increasingly included human nutrition work, using mainly stable isotopes to identify micronutrient malnutrition risk and monitor dietary improvement programmes.
158. IAEA nutrition programmes focused on iron, vitamin A, and iodine because deficiencies are common in developing countries, treatment is known, and effectiveness can be measured unambiguously.
159. Iron deficiency causes anaemia in about 50% of small children under four and 60% of women of childbearing age in developing countries, partly because vegetarian diets lack easily absorbed iron.
160. Stable or radioactive isotopes measure iron bioavailability and iron status, helping show which dietary changes best reduce anaemia and the impact of dietary enrichment.
161. Severe vitamin A deficiency afflicts about three million children and milder deficiency threatens 230 million; it can cause blindness, infectious disease vulnerability, and possibly increased mother-to-child AIDS transmission.
162. IAEA programmes support isotope measurement of vitamin A bioavailability and status, similar to iron-deficiency work.
163. Isotope techniques also help determine protein and amino acid requirements of undernourished children, monitor breast feeding and maternal energy requirements, evaluate dietary intervention programmes like school breakfasts, and explore causes of osteoporosis.
164. Ionizing radiation is valuable for bacteriological sterilization of heat-sensitive medical appliances such as surgical dressings, sutures, catheters, syringes, and graft tissues like bone, nerve, fascia, dura, and cardiac valves.
165. Chemical sterilization leaves hazardous residues, so the IAEA has played a key role in establishing radiation sterilization facilities in developing Member States in Asia, Latin America, Africa, and Europe.
166. Environmental pollution is a growing concern worldwide; nuclear analytical techniques provide useful, sensitive tools for research on non-radioactive pollutants and monitoring their environmental levels.
167. For many years, the IAEA helped nuclear centres in developing countries research non-radioactive pollutants such as toxic heavy metals (mercury, lead) and pesticide residues.
168. Recent IAEA-supported programmes focused on urban air pollution, especially airborne particles below 10 microns that penetrate deep into lungs and are thought to cause tens of thousands of premature deaths yearly in large cities.
169. The IAEA helped establish a unique network of centres in more than 30 mainly developing countries to collect and analyse airborne particles, identify major pollution sources, and obtain comparative pollution data.
170. Since 1958, isotope hydrology and uranium resource development were the main IAEA earth-science subjects, though uranium’s importance receded since the late 1980s when stocks rose, prices fell, and prospecting declined.
171. Isotope hydrology applications include measuring river, lake, and reservoir flow; groundwater flow rate and direction; underground water sources and pathways; groundwater recharge areas and rates; leakage and seepage losses; and sediment transport.
172. In the late 1950s, France, West Germany, Israel, Japan, the USA, and the UK began using radioactive tracers in water resource studies, encouraging the IAEA to enter the field in its first year, 1958.
173. Most early IAEA work involved injecting artificial radioisotopes as tracers for surface and groundwater, but naturally occurring isotopes, including stable water isotopes, largely replaced radioisotopes as analytical techniques improved.
174. In 1961, the IAEA and WMO launched a worldwide project measuring tritium, oxygen-18, and deuterium, establishing a global network to collect isotopic data and decipher nature’s ongoing tracer experiment.
175. Network meteorological stations rose from 100 at launch to 220 in 1962–63; after the 1963 Limited Test Ban Treaty, atmospheric tritium declined and station numbers fell.
176. Monitoring tritium and other isotopes in water molecules lets hydrologists determine water movement rates through the hydrological cycle, water origin and age, precipitation, evaporation, and infiltration rates.
177. In the 1980s, global warming concern spurred interest in accurate models of atmospheric water, water vapour, and greenhouse gas circulation, and in palaeoclimatology as a key to future changes; several countries established new national networks.
178. Water vapour is the chief greenhouse gas, maintaining Earth’s current temperature and making life possible; accurate information on its global cycle is valuable for climate change study.
179. In 1963, the IAEA began international symposia on isotope hydrology, usually every four years, with the ninth held in March 1995; it also trained developing-country scientists, with the fifth isotope hydrology course in 1995.
180. Since the mid-1980s, the IAEA has supported about 160 projects and trained more than 500 scientists to help governments use isotope hydrology techniques.
181. The IAEA’s first field project in 1960–61 was a hydrological study in Greece supporting an FAO irrigation project; later studies were carried out on all continents, often as a subcontractor to FAO or another UN agency under UNDP projects.
182. Recent isotope hydrology work covered groundwater pollution sources, nuclear waste disposal site suitability, atmospheric carbon dioxide concentration variations, and carbon sinks in oceans.
183. A regional groundwater study involving Egypt, Ethiopia, Morocco, and Senegal found Morocco’s groundwater contains much ancient palaeowater, disproving assumptions of recent replenishment and warning that current use rates will exhaust reserves.
184. A Philippines study showed Manila well water was not seepage from Laguna Lake as assumed, requiring additional deeper wells at new sites.
185. The IAEA helped five Caspian Sea countries use isotopes to determine why the sea level is rising and threatening cities and farmland, and used isotopes to measure Bangkok’s sinking groundwater level endangering ancient buildings.
186. Recent hydrological projects included El Salvador using stable isotopes to monitor geothermal resources and build a regional laboratory; Caracas, Venezuela, using isotopes to identify new well sites, define recharge mechanisms, and drill 50 new wells; and a Latin American regional project that showed $6 million in planned engineering work was unnecessary.
187. Uranium averages only two to four grams per tonne of rock; higher concentrations occur in certain strata, and it can be recovered as a by-product of gold mining in South Africa, copper mining in Chile, and phosphoric acid production in the USA.
188. After 1959, the IAEA broadened uranium technical assistance to geological studies, radiometric surveys, deposit identification, mining, yellow cake production, and uranium oxide processing; in the 1970s, over $600 million yearly was invested in uranium exploration and mine development.
189. The IAEA and OECD/NEA established a joint specialist group that produced common uranium reserve reporting standards, a uranium exploration techniques publication, and the periodic “Red Book” assessing free-market uranium resources, later expanded to former socialist countries.
190. Industrial isotope and radiation uses expanded from the 1955 Geneva Conference and 1959 Warsaw conference through polymer processing, rubber, plastics, paper, steel, tin, wood, sewage sludge, flue gas treatment, and non-destructive testing; by 1995–1998, a full-scale electron beam flue gas plant was expected in Poland, and IAEA non-destructive testing projects trained over 30,000 specialists from 1985–1995, helped Argentina establish a National Centre in 1972, and supported a Latin American regional project involving 18 countries by 1985.




191. The first Geneva Conference showed that nuclear science progress depended on full and free information exchange; scientists and technologists from 73 countries took part.
192. Led by the USA, participants cast off wartime nuclear information restrictions imposed by the 1945 Three Nation Agreed Declaration, the Baruch Plan, and the 1946 McMahon Act.
193. The Three Nation Declaration had required “reciprocal and enforceable safeguards acceptable to all nations” before exchanging fundamental scientific literature for peaceful ends.
194. Nevertheless, in 1955 the world proceeded with a massive exchange of nuclear information, especially on reactor design and construction, long before such safeguards existed.
195. Competitive declassification was partly a planned exercise to impress others with national nuclear manufacturing achievements.
196. The USA, UK, and USSR believed commercial nuclear power was near, and Geneva let them show their reactors were best; exhibitions made the meeting partly a trade fair.
197. The curtain of nuclear secrecy since 1939 was torn aside, for better or worse.
198. An inherent tension arose between spreading information that might help bomb designers and the IAEA Statute’s goal of promoting peaceful nuclear uses.
199. This tension later affected nuclear supplier policies, nuclear training and education, which countries were accepted, and even IAEA technical cooperation.
200. The Preparatory Commission’s Initial Programme stressed the IAEA’s role in promoting scientific and technical information exchange as an immediate service to Member States.
201. In March 1959, Director General Sterling Cole convened an expert panel to advise on Article VIII.C of the Statute.
202. Article VIII.C required the IAEA to ensure access to peaceful nuclear information furnished by Member States and to encourage such exchange.
203. Initial IAEA efforts included a technical library helped by US Atomic Energy Commission gifts, invited review articles, published reviews, scientific meetings, proceedings, and safety manuals.
204. In October 1960, the IAEA began its first scientific periodical, the quarterly Nuclear Fusion journal.
205. The IAEA’s first scientific conference was “The Application of Large Radiation Sources in Industry and Especially Chemical Processes,” held in Warsaw in September 1959.
206. By 1961, larger IAEA meetings classified as conferences, seminars, and symposia had risen to about 10–12 a year, remaining roughly at that level in the 1970s.
207. Smaller meetings also exchanged information, including Coordinated Research Programme meetings and specialist panels.
208. Larger meeting topics ranged broadly: small and medium power reactors, plasma physics and controlled fusion, tropical medicine, nuclear law, entomology, and tropical diseases.
209. The first international conference on plasma physics and controlled thermonuclear fusion was held in Salzburg in 1961.
210. Until 1988, when members’ terms were not renewed, the IAEA Scientific Advisory Committee selected the topics covered each year.
211. At UN request, the IAEA assumed scientific and technical responsibility for the third Geneva Conference in 1964 and co-sponsored the fourth with the UN in 1968.
212. In 1977, the IAEA convened a Salzburg conference as a follow-up to the four Geneva Conferences.
213. These very large, broad “Geneva-type” meetings were not repeated after 1977.
214. Specialized conferences and symposia declined to about six to eight a year in the 1980s as faster, more regular information exchange developed.
215. Specialist meetings still provided valuable intensive discussion or broad assessment, such as the September 1994 IAEA conference “The Nuclear Power Option.”
216. In 1959, the IAEA became a scientific publisher; by mid-1959 it had issued nine publications, including the first Safety Series manual, Safe Handling of Radioisotopes.
217. Early publications also included the first volume of the ten-volume Directory of Nuclear Reactors and the first volume of the two-volume International Directory of Radioisotopes and Labelled Compounds.
218. By 1961, the IAEA published papers and proceedings of about a dozen larger meetings, expert panel reports, Safety Series recommendations, special topic reviews, technical reports, Nuclear Fusion, and the IAEA Bulletin.
219. As its activities expanded, the IAEA became the leading international publisher on virtually every aspect of nuclear energy; by 1995 it issued nearly 150 scientific publications a year.
220. By the end of the 1960s, the IAEA library had become a valuable resource for IAEA programmes and Member States.
221. In 1970, its holdings included more than 30,000 books and nearly 120,000 technical reports.
222. In 1979, the IAEA library merged with UNIDO and other Vienna-based UN organization libraries to form the Vienna International Centre Library.
223. At the end of 1995, the Library had more than 115,000 books, about 1,200,000 documents, 250,000 technical reports, and subscriptions to about 4,000 journals, over half supporting IAEA work.
224. The Library stored and distributed information electronically and conventionally, subscribing to CD data files and providing access to external information files.
225. In 1963, the IAEA established a specialized unit for collecting and diffusing basic nuclear data.
226. The unit focused on nuclear cross-sections and other data fundamental to understanding nuclear fission and fission reactors.
227. It aimed to provide nuclear data to countries and regions such as India and Latin America not served by existing centres.
228. It collaborated with major nuclear data compilation centres in the USA, the USSR/Russian Federation, and the OECD/NEA centre, achieving worldwide nuclear data exchange among four centres.
229. In the second half of the 1960s, the IAEA launched the International Nuclear Information System (INIS) to promote exchange on all peaceful uses of nuclear energy.
230. INIS’s birth was difficult: Western Board members already had access to US Nuclear Science Abstracts and NEA services such as its nuclear data centre.
231. Cold War reluctance hindered comprehensive nuclear information exchange with the Soviet Union and its allies; one Governor even suggested one-for-one information exchange with the USSR.
232. Much credit for overcoming these obstacles went to the Canadian Director of the Division of Scientific and Technical Information and his US successor.
233. As early as 1962, an expert panel recommended that the IAEA help UNESCO efforts on mechanizing storage and retrieval of information and international science abstracting.
234. In 1965, Soviet and US experts outlined a scheme for an international information system covering expanding peaceful nuclear literature and meeting diverse national needs.
235. The IAEA convened expert panels to review consultants’ proposals and elaborate a detailed system design.
236. In 1968, an international team studied the system in detail and drew up a report to the Board.
237. The system was designed to provide machine-processed data on a user-selected nuclear topic.
238. It was decentralized: each participating State prepared and arranged input of all peaceful nuclear literature published or available in that State.
239. The IAEA arranged input of material produced by itself and other international organizations.
240. Each participating State designated a central point, with a national INIS Liaison Officer in charge; the centre did limited processing, recording bibliographic descriptions and key words on computer tape.
241. The IAEA processed incoming material, stored it, and distributed it to participating States and individual users.
242. INIS products included magnetic tapes or the printed bulletin INIS Atomindex with bibliographic descriptions and key words, plus cumulative indexes and microfiches with abstracts or full texts.
243. In February 1969, the Board approved INIS but imposed restrictions to control its growth and cost.
244. INIS began output in April/May 1970, distributing INIS Atomindex, computer tapes, and microfiches covering literature already reported.
245. In 1972, the Board decided INIS should cover all nuclear science information.
246. By the mid-1970s, 35 countries had agreed to participate, ensuring coverage of at least 90% of world nuclear publications.
247. In 1974–1976, INIS reached steady operation, processing 60,000–70,000 citations a year.
248. When US Nuclear Science Abstracts ceased in July 1976, INIS became the world’s only nuclear energy abstracting service and the only system with worldwide nuclear literature coverage.
249. In the late 1970s and 1980s, INIS provided a fully computerized service with direct on-line access; in the early 1990s it became available on CD-ROM, on-line, and in print, and served as a model for UNISIST and AGRIS.
250. By 1995, INIS’s 25th year, 94 States and 17 international organizations participated; its database exceeded 1.8 million references and grew by about 80,000 yearly, while its Clearinghouse had microfilmed over 345,000 non-conventional documents and begun storing full texts on CD-ROM.
